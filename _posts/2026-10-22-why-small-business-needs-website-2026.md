Someone needs a plumber, a birthday cake, or a dentist who is taking new patients. They open their phone, type a search and pick one. The search decides who gets the call.

You do not get a vote unless you appear in the results. That is the short version of why businesses need a website in 2026, and for a small business the website is where that vote gets counted.

This used to run the other way. A customer heard about you from a friend, drove past and walked in. Now the search happens first and the visit is the last step. [BrightLocal's Consumer Search Behavior research](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/consumer-search-behavior/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} from June 2026 found that 84% of consumers had searched online for a local business in the previous three months, and that 75% of them settled on a business in under half an hour. For 28%, the whole decision took less than five minutes.

Nobody is browsing. They are checking, then choosing, then moving on. Most never look past the first few options: 72% of consumers consider three businesses or fewer before they decide.

So your website is what gets opened once the shortlist starts forming. If it does not exist, the search ends at a map card and a phone number, and you are one of three. If it does exist, you are the one with the answers.

This guide covers the 2026 numbers, the five jobs only a website can do, what a build costs, and the pages that belong on it.

## Why local businesses need a website in 2026

Two numbers set the baseline.

**Only four in ten local businesses have a website.** [BrightLocal's Local Consumer Review Survey 2026](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}, based on a panel of 1,002 US adults, puts the share of local businesses with a dedicated website at 40%. The gap varies by trade. A September 2026 study of 23,164 US Google Business Profiles across 54 categories by Suff Digital found that 31% had no website link at all, and another 2.3% pointed only to a social page or a free Google or Square site. gtme.business sampled 295,783 listings across 40 trades, 50 metros and 4 countries in June 2026 and found 19.4% had no website listed at all.

**Having one tracks with a much stronger profile.** The Suff Digital dataset found a median of 2 Google reviews on profiles with no website, against 27 on profiles that link one. Weblish's 2025 Small Business Local Visibility and Trust Report, covering 1,732 businesses, framed the same gap in conversion terms. Strong profiles turned 6.2% of views into calls or direction clicks, weak ones turned 2.1%. Modelled on a business with 600 profile views a month, that is roughly 295 missed contact opportunities every year.

Then there is the shift that makes this a 2026 story rather than a 2020 story.

**Reviews now send people to websites.** In BrightLocal's 2026 survey, 54% of consumers said they visit a business's website after reading positive reviews. The same question in 2019 produced 32%. A review used to be the last step in the decision. Now it is the doorway.

**AI became a discovery channel in roughly twelve months.** The share of consumers using generative AI to find local business recommendations climbed from 6% in 2025 to 45% in 2026, according to BrightLocal. AI now ranks third among sources of local recommendations, behind Google and Facebook, and ahead of Yelp and TripAdvisor.

A quick test costs nothing. Search your business name, then search your main service plus your town. If you are not in either result set, fix that before spending anything on the site itself.

## What a website does that a map listing cannot

A Google Business Profile is a card. It holds your name, hours, phone number, category and rating. Claim it, complete it, keep the photos current. It is the foundation, not the answer.

Five jobs sit beyond what a card can carry.

### It answers the questions that kill the sale

Price. Service area. Whether you handle the specific problem the customer actually has. What it costs, how long it takes, whether you are insured.

BrightLocal's 2025 Consumer Search Behavior survey asked what consumers want after a local search. 85% said the presence of contact information matters, and 46% rated contact details and opening hours "very important", which placed those above price, proximity and product availability.

None of those answers fit in a map card. None of them should have to fit into a phone call.

### It gives AI assistants something to quote

When someone asks ChatGPT or Gemini for a recommendation near them, the model assembles an answer from pages it can read. A listing with twenty words and a star rating gives it very little to work with. A service page naming the problem, the area covered, the price range and the process gives it plenty.

BrightLocal's [September 2026 AI visibility study](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-ai-visibility-study/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} ran 200,085 localized, non-branded prompts across ChatGPT, Google AI Mode and AI Overviews, spread over 1,300 business locations, and counted 1.9 million AI citations. The headline finding was instability rather than inaccuracy. Repeat searches returned only 20% to 33% overlapping sets of businesses, and a business mentioned once came back in roughly half of later attempts. Between 71% and 80% of businesses appeared in half the attempts or fewer.

The businesses AI mentioned were the ones it could read something concrete about. The rest were not penalised. They were absent.

### It turns profile views into booked jobs

Profile views turn into actions, and website clicks are consistently the largest share of them. First Page Sage's 2026 click data, built on 41.2 million clicks across 1,184 domains, put profile actions at 47% website visits, 38% direction requests and 15% calls.

Direction requests are a dead end for a service business. A phone call is a dead end for anything with a longer sales conversation. A website visit is the only one of the three where a customer can look at your work, read your pricing and book at 10pm without troubling you.

### It owns the customer relationship

Your email list, your booking calendar, your customer history, your follow-up reminders. None of that lives on a platform that can change an algorithm, cap your reach or close an account.

### It survives platform changes

Instagram can take your reach to zero by changing a setting. A listing can be suspended. Platforms have been doing this to small businesses for a decade. A domain you own does not have that problem.

Try this week: open your profile on a phone and tap your own website link. If it leads nowhere, you are losing the 47% of profile actions that turn into site visits.

## Where customers go when you have no site

The most expensive loss is not the customer who never finds you. It is the one who found you through a referral and then went elsewhere.

A friend mentions your shop to someone else. That person searches your name, finds no site, and sees a social page with fourteen posts from 2021 and no phone number. The conclusion is not that you are busy. The conclusion is that you might not be real, so the next result gets the booking.

You had already earned that attention. The website is what converts it.

The other losses are quieter. You cannot answer a pricing question without a call. You cannot show a portfolio. You cannot prove you have done the job before. Each of those becomes a reason to choose the competitor whose site answers it in ten seconds.

Search your own business name as a stranger would. Whatever appears on that first screen is your shop window, and most people are judging it in a couple of seconds.

## Why businesses need a website, not just a social media page

Social media is not a substitute, and it is not going to become one.

You do not own it. Algorithms change, reach collapses, accounts get suspended. It has happened to other businesses and it will happen to yours.

Google treats a Facebook page as a listing, not as a website. Search for your service and your page will not surface where a site would.

A consumer who lands on a professional site reads it differently from a feed. The site is evidence. The feed is thinner evidence.

Platforms also limit what you can show, how you collect a lead and how long a customer can stay on a page you control.

Use social media to start conversations and send people to your site. Never the other way around.

Do this now: put your hours, your phone number and one clear next step into the link in bio of your main profile. Then point that link at your own domain rather than a platform page.

## Five ways a small website turns into a liability

### Built in one weekend, then ignored

A site with three services listed and a phone number from 2024 is worse than no site, because it looks like an answer and delivers nothing. Search engines will still index the stale pages, and your competitors' fresher pages will outrank yours.

### Designed for a desktop and barely usable on a phone

Most local searches happen on a phone. If the phone number is not tappable, the booking form needs a magnifying glass, or the text is set at 12 pixels, you lose the visitor before they read a word. Mobile first is not a design preference at this point, it is a baseline cost, and we covered the reasoning in [why you should go mobile first](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/03/why-go-mobile-first.html).

### Slow on a mid-range Android phone

Most of your customers are not on the newest hardware. A site that takes five seconds to show its main content on a three-year-old phone will lose people in the four seconds before it loads.

### Impossible to reach

No phone number in the header, no contact form, no map link, no hours. Visitors who cannot reach you in one tap go to the business that makes it easy.

### Silent about proof

A site with no reviews, no case studies and no photographs of finished work asks the customer to take a risk on the strength of a stock image. Trust is built out loud in [our guide on raising your website trust score](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/15/how-to-increase-website-trust-score.html), and it takes concrete proof rather than adjectives.

Try this week: open your site on a mid-range Android phone using mobile data rather than wifi, and time how long the main content takes to appear.

## What a website costs in 2026

The honest answer is that it costs less than most owners expect, and more than the cheapest quote implies.

SiteGround's 2026 guide, citing Clutch's 2026 survey, reports that 61% of small business buyers spent under $10,000 on their most recent website and 84% spent under $20,000. GoodFirms' survey found 51.6% of agencies quoting between $3,000 and $15,000 for small-scale sites.

A 2026 pricing census by 99creatix, which compiled first-party price lists from 34 providers that actually publish prices, put the median US custom small-business site at $7,000 across six samples, ranging from $4,000 to $18,000. The median basic brochure site was $799.

| Build path | Typical 2026 cost | What you get | The catch |
|---|---|---|---|
| DIY builder (Wix, Squarespace, Shopify) | $16 to $50 per month | A live site this weekend | You own the updates too |
| Self-hosted WordPress | $100 to $500 a year plus domain | Control over every layer | Needs some technical comfort |
| AI website builder | Free to about $50 per month | A fast first draft | Publishing and a custom domain usually need a paid plan |
| Freelancer | $1,500 to $8,000 | Custom design without agency overhead | Turnaround time and aftercare vary |
| Small studio or agency | $6,000 to $35,000 and up | Strategy, design, development, support | Scope creep is real, so fix the scope in writing |

The cheapest path is not always the cheapest total. A $20 per month builder that nobody on your team can edit is more expensive than a $2,000 build you can maintain, because you will pay someone else every time a price changes. Price the ongoing hours before you pick a route.

Write down what the site would need to do for you, then price that scope rather than a page count. The two come out very different.

## The nine pages a small business website needs

1. **Home:** a page that says what you do, where you work and how to reach you.
2. **One page per service:** "emergency lockout" and "key replacement" are different searches with different intent.
3. **Service area:** the towns, suburbs or postal areas you cover, on their own page if you cover several.
4. **About the business:** real names, real credentials, and how long you have been open.
5. **Proof:** reviews, testimonials and case studies with numbers in them.
6. **Gallery:** before and after shots, finished work, the shop front, the team.
7. **Pricing:** a starting price or a range removes more hesitation than any adjective will.
8. **FAQ:** the questions you answer on the phone every single week.
9. **Contact and booking:** a form, a phone number, your hours and a way to book, repeated on every page.
