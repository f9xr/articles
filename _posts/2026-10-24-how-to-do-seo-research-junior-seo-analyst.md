Your manager says, "Can you do some SEO research on this client by Friday?" You nod, open a spreadsheet, and then stare at it for ten minutes.

SEO research is the part of the job nobody explains properly. Tutorials hand you a tool list and wish you luck, but they rarely show you how to think, what to look for, or how to turn a pile of data into a plan a business owner can act on.

This guide covers how to do SEO research the way a junior analyst should, and it works just as well for business owners, startup founders and local shop owners who want to understand what good research looks like before they hire anyone.

Search is no longer ten blue links. According to [Ahrefs' 2026 statistics roundup](https://ahrefs.com/blog/seo-statistics/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}, Google AI Overviews are linked to a 58% lower average click-through rate for the top-ranking organic page. The same report says 96.55% of all pages get zero organic traffic from Google. Organic search drives about 53% of website traffic, according to BrightEdge data cited by Colorlib. Search matters. It rewards people who do their homework.

## How to do SEO research: what the job involves

SEO research is the process of finding out what your customers search for, who already answers those searches, and what it will take for your website to earn visibility on Google, Bing, Google Maps, and AI search engines like ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and Perplexity.

A good junior analyst answers five questions:

1. Who are we trying to reach? (audience and location)
2. What are they searching for? (keywords and questions)
3. What do they really want when they search it? (search intent)
4. Who is winning right now, and why? (competitors and SERP features)
5. What should we do first? (prioritized recommendations)

If your research does not end with a clear answer to question five, it is a data dump.

---

## Step 0: Understand the business before you touch a tool

This is the step most beginners skip.

Before you open Semrush, Ahrefs or Google Search Console, spend an hour learning the business. Ask the owner or account manager:

- What do you sell, and which product or service makes the most profit?
- Who is your ideal customer? Where are they located?
- What questions do customers ask you on calls, WhatsApp or in person?
- Who do you think your top three competitors are?
- What does a win look like: calls, form fills, store visits, bookings, sales?

A dental clinic in a mid-size city may think its goal is "rank for dentist." The real money might be in "root canal treatment cost" and "emergency dentist open Sunday." You only find that out by talking to the business first.

Record the answers in a one-page brief. Every keyword, competitor and recommendation should connect back to it.

## Step 1: Keyword research

Keyword research is the foundation. Here is a way to do it without drowning in data.

### Start with seed keywords

Seed keywords are the plain-language terms that describe the business. For a bakery, that could be "birthday cake," "custom cakes" and "eggless cake." Write 10 to 20 of them. Add the city or neighborhood for local businesses.

### Use free sources first

You can build a strong keyword list before spending a rupee or a dollar.

- [Google Search Console](https://support.google.com/webmasters){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} shows the real queries your site already appears for. This is gold for existing websites.
- Google Autocomplete suggests phrases as you type a seed keyword. Note every variation.
- People Also Ask boxes are great for question-style keywords and FAQ ideas.
- Related searches at the bottom of the results page reveal adjacent topics.
- [Google Trends](https://trends.google.com){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} shows seasonality and rising topics.
- Google Keyword Planner gives volume ranges and related terms, even without a big ad budget.
- Customer language from reviews, comments, forum threads and Google Business Profile Q&A shows how real people talk.

### Judge every keyword on four things

Do not chase volume alone. Score each keyword on these factors:

| Factor | What to ask | Where to check |
|---|---|---|
| Search volume | Do enough people search this? | Keyword Planner, Semrush, Ahrefs |
| Keyword difficulty | Can this site realistically rank? | Semrush, Ahrefs, manual SERP review |
| Search intent | What does the searcher want? | Read the top 10 results |
| Business value | Will this lead to revenue? | Your client brief |

### A simple keyword sheet

| Keyword | Intent | Priority | Suggested page |
|---|---|---|---|
| emergency dentist near me | Local / transactional | High | Emergency dental service page |
| root canal treatment cost | Commercial | High | Pricing guide blog post |
| is root canal painful | Informational | Medium | FAQ section or blog |
| best dentist in [city] | Local / commercial | High | Homepage and Google Business Profile |
| dental clinic open Sunday | Local / transactional | Medium | Contact page and GBP hours |

These are examples. Real volumes and difficulty scores depend on your market and tool.

Group keywords into topic clusters, such as "root canal," "teeth whitening" and "braces," instead of treating each keyword as its own page. Search engines and AI systems both understand topics better than single phrases. When you are ready to write, this guide to [writing SEO friendly articles](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/09/how-to-write-seo-friendly-articles.html) covers how to place those terms without stuffing them.

## Step 2: Search intent and SERP analysis

Search intent is the reason behind a search. Google and AI engines are good at matching intent, so if your page does not match it, you will not rank no matter how well you write.

There are four common intent types:

- Informational: "how to do SEO research"
- Navigational: "Google Search Console login"
- Commercial: "best SEO tools for small business"
- Transactional: "hire local SEO expert"

### How to read a SERP in five minutes

Search your keyword in an incognito window and note:

1. What content types rank? Blog posts, product pages, videos, maps, forums?
2. Is there an AI Overview, featured snippet, People Also Ask box or local map pack?
3. How long and how deep are the top pages?
4. Which brands or sites show up again and again?
5. What are they missing that you can answer better?

If the top 10 results are all product pages and you plan to write a blog post, you have a mismatch. Fix the plan before you write a word.

## Step 3: Competitor research

Your business competitors and your search competitors are not always the same. A local plumber might compete with a directory like Justdial or a national marketplace for the same searches.

To find the real ones, search your top 10 keywords and list every domain that appears at least three times.

### What to look for

- **Top pages:** Which URLs bring them the most organic traffic?
- **Content gaps:** Which topics do they cover that you do not?
- **Backlinks:** Which websites link to them? Are there easy wins like local directories, partners or news mentions?
- **On-page patterns:** How do they structure titles, headings, FAQs and schema?
- **Trust signals:** Reviews, author bios, case studies, certifications.

Backlink research also tells you what is realistic. If competitors have 500 referring domains and your client has 12, plan a longer road and say so in your report.

## Step 4: Local SEO research

Local businesses have an extra layer to research, and many ignore it. Ahrefs' compilation notes that 58% of companies do not optimize for local search, which means a careful analyst can create an advantage.

### Local research checklist

- **Google Business Profile:** Is it claimed, verified and complete? Check categories, services, photos, hours and posts.
- **NAP consistency:** Is the business name, address and phone number identical across the website, GBP and directories?
- **Map pack competitors:** Who shows up for "service + city" searches, and why? Compare review counts, ratings and categories.
- **Reviews:** Look at volume, recency, and how the owner responds.
- **Local citations:** Check listings on relevant directories and industry sites.
- **Location pages:** Does the site have useful pages for each service area, not thin copies with swapped city names?

## Step 5: Technical and on-page basics

You do not need to be a developer, but you do need to spot problems that block rankings. Run a crawl with a tool like Screaming Frog or Semrush Site Audit, then check the basics. Google's [Search Central documentation](https://developers.google.com/search/docs){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} is the reference for every item below.

| Area | What to check | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Indexing | Pages in Search Console, noindex tags, robots.txt | Pages that are not indexed cannot rank |
| Speed and Core Web Vitals | PageSpeed Insights, Search Console reports | Slow pages lose visitors and rankings |
| Mobile usability | Mobile layout, tap targets, text size | Most local searches happen on phones |
| Site structure | Clean URLs, internal links, breadcrumbs | Helps crawlers and users find content |
| Titles and meta | Unique, clear, around 50 to 60 characters | Affects click-through rate |
| Headings | One H1, logical H2 and H3 | Helps readers and AI systems parse content |
| Schema markup | LocalBusiness, FAQ, Article, Organization | Gives machines clear context |
| Images | Alt text, file size, descriptive names | Supports accessibility and image search |

For schema, start with [Schema.org](https://schema.org){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} and match the type to the page, not the other way around. Our [monthly website audit checklist](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/03/monthly-website-audit-checklist.html) is a good routine to follow.

## Step 6: How to do SEO research for AI search (AEO)

This is the new part of the job, and it is where a junior analyst can stand out quickly.

Answer Engine Optimization (AEO) is about making your content easy for AI tools like ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Perplexity and Google AI Overviews to understand, trust and cite. The research looks a little different.

### A simple AI visibility test

1. Write 10 to 15 questions your customers actually ask.
2. Run each one through ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and Perplexity.
3. Record whether the business is mentioned, which competitors are mentioned, and which sources are cited.
4. Note the format of the answers: lists, definitions, comparisons, step-by-step guides.
5. Repeat monthly to track change.

### What to look for

- **Cited sources:** Which sites get quoted most often in your niche. These are your real competition for AI visibility.
- **Content freshness:** Ahrefs reports that AI-cited content is about 25.7% fresher on average than pages ranking in regular Google results, so update dates and facts matter.
- **Quotable answers:** Short definitions, tables and FAQs are easier for AI systems to lift.
- **Entity clarity:** Is it obvious who the business is, where it operates, what it offers and who runs it?
- **Trust signals:** Author names, reviews, citations, editorial policies and consistent business details all help.

## Step 7: Turn research into a report people will read

Business owners do not want 80 pages of screenshots. They want to know what to do, in what order, and why.

### Use an impact vs effort matrix

| Priority | Impact | Effort | Example action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Do first | High | Low | Fix GBP categories, rewrite weak title tags |
| Plan next | High | High | Build service pages, create a content cluster, earn quality backlinks |
| Quick extras | Low | Low | Add alt text, fix broken links |
| Skip for now | Low | High | Chasing hard keywords with no business value |

### What a good report includes

- A one-paragraph summary in plain English
- Top 10 keyword opportunities with the page each one should target
- A competitor snapshot: who they are, why they win, where they are weak
- Local SEO findings
- Technical issues ranked by severity
- AI visibility findings
- A 30, 60 and 90 day action plan

Tie each recommendation to a number the client already cares about, whether that is calls, form fills or booked jobs. Our guide to [SEO metrics beyond rankings](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/19/seo-metrics-beyond-rankings.html) explains which ones belong in a report and which are noise.

Always say what you could not verify. Being honest about limits builds trust faster than pretending the data is perfect.

## Tools every junior SEO analyst should know

| Task | Free option | Paid option |
|---|---|---|
| Real search data | Google Search Console | Not needed, use GSC first |
| Traffic and behavior | Google Analytics 4 | Not needed to start |
| Keyword ideas | Google Keyword Planner, Autocomplete, Google Trends | Semrush, Ahrefs |
| Competitor and backlink data | Limited free checks | Ahrefs, Semrush |
| Technical crawling | Screaming Frog (free up to 500 URLs) | Semrush Site Audit |
| Speed testing | PageSpeed Insights | Not needed to start |
| Local research | Google Business Profile, Google Maps | Local rank trackers |
| AI search checks | ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Perplexity (free tiers) | AI visibility trackers |

Start free and add paid tools only when a real question demands one.

## Common mistakes junior analysts make

- Chasing volume. A keyword with 50 searches a month and high buying intent can beat one with 5,000 vague searches.
- Skipping intent. Writing the wrong type of page for the keyword.
- Copying competitors. Research should show you gaps, not tell you to clone someone else.
- Ignoring local signals. For a neighborhood business, GBP often matters more than the blog.
- Trusting one tool. Tools disagree. Cross-check with Search Console and the live SERP.
- No follow-through. Research that never becomes a task list does nothing for the client.

## Key takeaways

- SEO research starts with the business, not the tool. Learn the goals, audience and location first.
- Use free sources like Search Console, Autocomplete, People Also Ask and Google Trends before paying for anything.
- Score keywords by volume, difficulty, intent and business value together.
- Read the live search results to understand intent before planning any page.
- Study real competitors, including directories and marketplaces, not just the businesses you know.
- For local businesses, Google Business Profile, reviews and NAP consistency are core research areas.
- Test your visibility in ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and Perplexity every month.
- Finish every research project with a prioritized, plain-English action plan.

## Conclusion

SEO research is not magic, and it is not about owning the most expensive tools. It is a repeatable habit: understand the business, find what people search, read the results with a skeptical eye, study who is winning, and turn all of it into a short list of actions.

If you are a junior analyst, run this workflow on one real website this month. If you are a business owner, use it as a checklist to judge the quality of any SEO report you receive.
