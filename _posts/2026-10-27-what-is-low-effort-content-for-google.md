Google has never been short on rules about content quality. What it was short on was a plain answer to a simple question: what does low effort content actually look like?

In early October 2026, that changed. Google updated its <a href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Creating helpful, reliable, people-first content</a> guidance and spelled out four things its quality raters judge in a page's main content: effort, originality, talent or skill, and accuracy. The page now shows a last updated date of October 5, 2026.

Days earlier, the September 2026 spam update had already put scaled, low value pages under the spotlight.

If you run a small business, a startup, or a local service company, this matters. You do not need to be a content factory to fit the pattern. A thin service page, a blog post nobody fact checked, or fifty city pages that only differ by the city name can all fit the description.

> **Quick answer:** Low effort content is web content made with little human work, little original thought, and little added value compared with what already exists on the topic. Common examples are mass produced AI text with no human review, reworded copies of competitor pages, and templated pages built only to catch searches. Google's quality raters can score these pages Low or Lowest.

## What changed in early October 2026

Google added new text to its helpful content page. Three additions matter.

Main content now has a definition. It is any part of a page that directly helps it do its job. That includes text and media, plus calculators, tools, site search, user contributions, and even page titles when they serve the page's purpose.

Four attributes now guide raters: effort, originality, talent or skill, and accuracy.

Google also warns about fake creators. It describes invented author names, AI generated headshots, and made up credentials as deceptive.

Google also refreshed its generative AI guidance. It stresses manual fact checking before publishing and reminds creators that models predict likely words rather than retrieve facts.

One caveat. Quality raters do not set rankings directly. Their ratings help Google test and improve its systems. Treat the guidelines as a description of what Google rewards, not a switch you can flip.

## What is low effort content? A plain English definition

Low effort content is content where very little work went into making it. Nobody researched it, nobody added a fresh angle, nobody checked it, and a reader learns nothing they could not get from the top five results already ranking.

Google's <a href="https://guidelines.raterhub.com/searchqualityevaluatorguidelines.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Search Quality Rater Guidelines</a> put it in two tiers:

* Low: some of the main content is reused, but there is at least a minimal attempt to curate, edit, or add to it.
* Lowest: almost all of the main content is copied or paraphrased with no effort and no added value.

The related spam idea is called scaled content abuse. Google's <a href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">spam policies</a> describe it as creating large amounts of content with little effort or originality and no editing or manual curation, mainly to benefit the site owner rather than the reader. Generative AI is named as one example of an automated tool that can be used this way. It is not the only one.

Low effort is not the same as short. A 3,000 word article stuffed with filler can be lower effort than a 400 word page with a price list, a photo, and a direct answer.

## The four tests: effort, originality, talent or skill, accuracy

The table below shows how each attribute works on a typical business website.

| Test | What Google is asking | Low effort looks like | High effort looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Effort | How much human work, or work on the systems behind the page, went into it? | Bulk AI text published without editing. Pages generated from a feed. | Original analysis, a custom tool, a calculator you built, photos you took. |
| Originality | Does it offer information or views not already on other sites? | A reworded version of the top three results. | Your own data, your own case study, your own process. |
| Talent or skill | Is it made well enough to give visitors a satisfying experience? | Rambling text, broken page tools, filler that hides the answer. | Clear writing, a working form, a well shot video. |
| Accuracy | Is it factually correct? | Confident claims with no source, outdated prices, invented stats. | Checked facts, cited sources, expert review on serious topics. |

Two details are easy to miss. Giving credit to another source does not replace original effort; linking to a study does not make a copied page useful. And the accuracy bar is higher for topics that can affect health, money, or safety. If you write about legal, tax, medical, or financial matters, review your pages with extra care. ## Examples of low effort content on business websites

These patterns show up most often when we audit small business sites.

### Templated location pages

"Plumber in Area A," "Plumber in Area B," and "Plumber in Area C," all with the same paragraph and one word swapped. This is the classic local SEO shortcut, and the classic problem.

### Bulk AI blog posts

Thirty posts a month, no author, no editing, and no examples. Google's own guidance treats large volumes of generative AI text without human oversight as a little to no effort pattern.

### Spun competitor content

You read a competitor's guide, ask a tool to rewrite it, and publish. Paraphrasing alone is what the Lowest rating describes.

### Filler that buries the answer

Long intros, repeated points, and padded sections that push the useful part down the page. The reader came for a price or a how-to, and you made them scroll.

### Reposts and embeds with no commentary

A page that embeds a video or repeats a social post with nothing added by you. Raters treat this repackaged material as Low quality.

### Fake expertise

Invented author names, stock photo doctors, or credentials that cannot be verified. This is a trust problem on top of an effort problem.

If any of these sound familiar, do not panic. Most can be fixed.

## Is AI generated content automatically low effort?

No. This misunderstanding causes more confusion than any other part of the update.

Google's guidance focuses on the value of the content, not the tool used to make it. What counts against you is publishing AI text at scale with no human judgment, no checking, and nothing original added.

A workflow that holds up:

* A human decides what the reader needs.
* A human supplies the first hand material: photos, numbers, quotes, process notes.
* AI helps with structure, drafting, or editing.
* A human fact checks every claim and signs off.

If the value comes from your experience and your editing, the tool matters much less. We wrote a practical walkthrough in [how to de-AI-slop your website](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/28/how-to-de-ai-slop-your-website.html).

## How to check your website for low effort content

You do not need expensive software. Take your ten most important pages and ask these questions.

1. If this page vanished, would searchers lose anything? If the answer is no, the page adds nothing.
2. Could a competitor have written it without knowing your business? If yes, it has no original input.
3. Is there anything here that only you could say? Prices, process, photos, results, local knowledge.
4. Who wrote it, and can a reader verify that? A name, a role, a profile.
5. Has every number and claim been checked? Look for outdated figures and unsourced stats.
6. Does the useful part appear quickly? Or is it buried under filler?

Score each page honestly. Anything that fails three or more questions goes on your fix list.

## Practical fixes for business owners, startups, and local businesses

Once you know which pages are weak, you have three options for each one: improve, merge, or remove. Here is how to improve.

### Add first hand proof

Photos of your own work, a customer story with permission, the steps your team follows. Nothing else separates you from a generic page as quickly.

### Name the person behind the page

Add an author name, a short bio, and a link to a profile. If you run a clinic, firm, or workshop, name the person who reviewed the page. Never use made up experts.

### Publish your own numbers

Even small data counts. How long does a typical job take? What does a common mistake cost? What did your last 50 customers ask about most? Original data gives readers and AI systems something to cite.

### Merge near-duplicate pages

If you have five posts that say roughly the same thing, combine them into one strong page and redirect the others. Less content, more value.

### Remove what cannot be saved

Some pages exist only because someone once wanted more pages. Deleting or noindexing them is a legitimate fix. Our guide on [reviving dead content with systematic audits](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/20/reviving-dead-content-systematic-audits.html) shows how to decide.

### Fact check and improve usability

Every statistic needs a source and a date. Every price needs a date showing when it was last checked. Every claim about law, health, or money needs a qualified reviewer. Then check the page itself. A working quote form, a pricing calculator, a clear map, or a short checklist counts as main content too.

## Before and after: a local service page

One example shows how a page changes when effort goes in.

| Element | Low effort version | High effort version |
|---|---|---|
| Headline | "Best AC Repair Services" | "AC Repair Costs and Timelines: What 50 Recent Jobs Showed Us" |
| Body | 300 generic words about cooling | Price ranges, common faults, typical repair time, and a clear guide on when to replace it |
| Images | Stock photo of a smiling technician | Photos of your team and jobs, with descriptive alt text |
| Author | None | Named senior technician, with a short bio |
| Proof | "Trusted by many customers" | Three short customer stories and links to your Google reviews |
| Tools | None | Simple cost estimator and a booking form |

The second version takes more work. It also gives a reader a reason to choose you, and it gives Google something specific to evaluate.

## What this means for AI search

Answer engines pull from the web to build their responses. They tend to favor pages that state something specific, clearly, and in a way that is easy to lift into an answer. A page that repeats what ten other pages already say gives them no reason to pick yours.

You cannot see exactly how each system chooses its sources, so treat this as sensible practice rather than a guarantee:

* Lead sections with a direct answer, then expand.
* Use clear headings and short paragraphs.
* Include original facts, figures, and named sources.
* Show who wrote the page and why they are qualified.
* Keep key business details such as name, address, services, and prices consistent everywhere.
