Picture two accounting firms in your city. Both publish a blog post on the same tax deadline. The advice is almost identical.

One firm has a page that says who writes its articles, who checks the numbers, how often old posts get reviewed, and where to report a mistake. The other firm has nothing. Just a nice logo and a blog.

Now put yourself in the shoes of a nervous reader. Or a quality rater at Google. Or an AI assistant deciding which source feels safe to lean on. Which firm looks more dependable?

That small, unglamorous page is called an **editorial policy page**. Most business owners skip it because it does not sell anything. Yet it quietly answers the one question every visitor and every search system keeps asking: *Can I trust you?*

In this guide, you will learn what an editorial policy page is, how it supports your E-E-A-T, what to put on it, and how to publish one this week, even if your team is just two people and a laptop.

## What Is an Editorial Policy Page?

An **editorial policy page** is a public web page that explains how your business researches, writes, reviews, updates, and corrects the content it publishes. It names who is responsible, which sources you trust, how you use AI tools, and how readers can report errors. It works as a trust signal that supports E-E-A-T.

Think of it as the "house rules" of your content. A restaurant has a hygiene rating on the door. An editorial policy is the same idea for your website: proof that there is a process behind what you say.

You may also hear it called:

- Editorial standards
- Content policy
- Editorial guidelines
- Publishing principles
- Content integrity statement

The name matters less than the substance. What counts is that a reader, a quality rater, or a crawler can find it in one click and understand your process in two minutes.

### Editorial Policy vs. Other Trust Pages

Many owners confuse these pages. They do different jobs, and the best sites have all of them working together.

| Page | Main question it answers | Typical content |
|---|---|---|
| **About page** | Who are you? | Story, team, mission, credentials, ownership |
| **Editorial policy** | How do you create and check your content? | Research steps, review process, sourcing rules, AI use, update schedule |
| **Correction policy** | What happens when you get something wrong? | How to report errors, response time, how fixes are noted |
| **Ethics or fact-checking policy** | What standards do you refuse to break? | Source standards, conflicts of interest, data integrity |
| **Transparency or ownership page** | Who pays for this and who is behind it? | Funding, ownership, affiliate and ad disclosures |
| **Author page** | Who wrote this specific piece? | Bio, experience, qualifications, links to their work |

If you only have time for one new page this month, start with the editorial policy. It links naturally to everything else in that table.

## E-E-A-T in 60 Seconds

Before we connect the dots, a quick refresher. E-E-A-T stands for **Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, and Trust**. Google's Search Quality Rater Guidelines use it to describe what a high-quality page looks like.

A few facts worth knowing:

- Google added the extra "E" for **Experience** in December 2022, turning E-A-T into E-E-A-T. It asks whether the creator has real, first-hand involvement with the topic, such as actually using a product or visiting a place.
- Google says **Trust is the most important** part of the family. The other three contribute to trust, and a page does not have to show every one of them.
- E-E-A-T is **not a single ranking factor** and there is no E-E-A-T score. It is a framework that raters use to evaluate search quality, and Google's own guidance encourages creators to use it to self-assess their content.

That last point is important, and it keeps this article honest. An editorial policy page is not a magic switch that pushes you to position one. What it does is give people (and machines) visible, checkable evidence that your content comes from a responsible process. That is exactly the type of evidence the E-E-A-T framework is built around.

For a full walkthrough of the framework, see our [Google E-E-A-T checklist](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/04/google-e-e-a-t-checklist-every-seo-should-bookmark.html).

## How an Editorial Policy Page Helps Your E-E-A-T

Here is where the page earns its place. We walk through each pillar.

### Experience: Show That Real People Did Real Work

Experience is about first-hand knowledge. Your editorial policy can state that articles are written by team members who actually do the work, and that examples come from real projects, real client situations (with permission), or hands-on testing.

For a local business, this might sound like: "Our guides are written by our own technicians and reviewed by the owner. We only recommend methods we have used on actual jobs."

That one sentence tells a visitor you are not a content farm.

### Expertise: Make Qualifications Easy to Find

Expertise is about knowledge and skill. Your policy can explain who is allowed to write or review which topics. A clinic can say that health content is reviewed by a licensed doctor. A CA firm can say that tax and compliance content is checked by a practicing Chartered Accountant before it goes live.

Link from the policy to your author pages, so the claim is backed by a real profile.

### Authoritativeness: Prove You Are a Recognized Source

Authority builds slowly, through reputation and mentions from others. An editorial policy cannot create that by itself. But it supports authority by showing you cite primary sources such as official documentation, government data, and original research, rather than recycled blog posts.

Sites that behave like serious publishers tend to look like serious publishers. A documented standard is part of the behavior.

### Trust: The Big One

Trust is where this page does the heaviest lifting. Trust comes from accuracy, honesty, transparency, and accountability. An editorial policy touches every one of those:

- **Accuracy:** you describe how facts are verified.
- **Honesty:** you disclose how AI tools are used and whether content is sponsored or affiliate-based.
- **Transparency:** you say who owns the site and who reviews content.
- **Accountability:** you promise a way to report mistakes and a timeline for fixing them.

Our guide on [how to increase your website trust score](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/15/how-to-increase-website-trust-score.html) covers the wider trust toolkit. The editorial policy is one of the most affordable pieces in it.

### Quick Summary: Pillar by Pillar

| E-E-A-T pillar | What the editorial policy can show | Example line you could publish |
|---|---|---|
| **Experience** | Content comes from hands-on work | "Guides are based on jobs our team has completed." |
| **Expertise** | Qualified people write and review | "Medical topics are reviewed by a licensed physician." |
| **Authoritativeness** | Reliable sourcing standards | "We prefer official documentation and primary research." |
| **Trust** | Accuracy checks, AI disclosure, corrections | "Report an error and we reply within 2 working days." |

## Does an Editorial Policy Page Help SEO and AI Search?

Be practical about this and avoid hype.

**For Google Search:** Google has said its systems prioritize helpful, reliable, people-first content, and its self-assessment guidance asks whether readers can find clear sourcing, evidence of expertise, and background about the author or the site. An editorial policy page answers those questions in one place. It is a supporting signal, not a ranking button.

**For AI search engines:** Tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, and Perplexity do not publish a checklist for choosing which pages to cite, so nobody can promise you a citation. What we can say is that answer engines work better with content that is easy to verify: clear authorship, named sources, visible update dates, and consistent facts across your site. A well-written policy page makes your site look like a source that is safe to quote. It also gives AI systems a clean, factual page to read when they are trying to understand who you are and how you work.

**For real people:** This is the most reliable benefit. A visitor who is about to hand over money, health details, or a phone number will often look for proof that your business is legitimate. A transparent policy page lowers that hesitation.

## A Real Example: How F9XR Articles Does It

Theory is easier to follow with a live example, so here is how our own [Editorial Policy](https://f9xr.org/articles/press/editorial-policy.html) is structured. Use it as a model, then adapt the depth to your size.

- **A clear content pipeline.** Every article moves through four stages: research, drafting, technical review, and editorial review.
- **A hybrid human and AI stance.** All content is reviewed by a human. AI tools may assist with tasks like grammar checks and outlines, but AI is never treated as the sole author, and disclosures are added when AI materially contributed.
- **Source standards.** Primary sources are preferred, and technical claims are checked against current documentation.
- **A review rhythm.** Time-sensitive articles are reviewed every 90 days, and the rest are reviewed yearly.
- **A corrections promise.** Reader-reported issues are acknowledged within 48 hours and resolved within 7 business days, with the process explained in the [Correction Policy](https://f9xr.org/articles/press/correction-policy.html).
- **Connected pages.** The policy links to the [Ethics and Fact-Checking Policy](https://f9xr.org/articles/press/ethics-policy.html), the [Transparency page](https://f9xr.org/articles/press/transparency.html), and [author pages](https://f9xr.org/articles/authors/f9xr-team/).

Notice what this example does well. It is specific. It uses numbers and timelines. It links to related pages instead of repeating them. And it is short enough that a busy reader can scan it.

You do not need a four-stage pipeline if you run a flower shop. You do need honest, specific statements you can actually keep.

## What to Include in Your Editorial Policy Page

Use this as your content checklist. Each item is a small section, and most can be two or three sentences.

### 1. Purpose and Scope

Say what the policy covers: blog posts, service pages, guides, social content, newsletters, and so on. One short paragraph is enough.

### 2. Who Creates and Reviews Your Content

Name roles, not just "our team." For example: "Articles are written by our service staff and reviewed by the owner before publishing." Link to author pages or bios.

### 3. Your Research and Sourcing Standards

Explain what you trust and what you avoid. Official documentation, government data, original research, and first-hand testing are strong choices. Mention how you handle statistics, quotes, and attribution.

### 4. Fact-Checking and Review Process

Describe the steps. Even a simple "draft, second-person check, publish" shows a process exists. For a deeper method, see our guide on [how to fact-check and audit your website](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/10/04/how-to-fact-check-audit-your-website.html).

### 5. AI Use and Disclosure

This section matters more every month. State plainly whether you use AI tools, for which tasks, and that a human reviews the final result. Avoid vague claims like "100 percent human" if it is not true. Honesty is the whole point. If your content ever feels generic, our post on [how to de-AI-slop your website](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/28/how-to-de-ai-slop-your-website.html) will help.

### 6. Corrections and Updates

Give a way to report errors (an email or form), a response time you can keep, and a rule for how corrections are shown on the page. Link to your correction policy if you have one.

### 7. Review Schedule

Say how often content gets rechecked. Quarterly for fast-changing topics and yearly for evergreen ones is a realistic rhythm for small teams. Add "Last reviewed" dates on the policy page itself.

### 8. Independence, Sponsorship, and Affiliate Disclosure

If you earn money from links, ads, or sponsored content, say so and explain that it does not change your recommendations. Link to your disclosure page.

### 9. Contact Details

Add an email, phone number, or contact form. A policy with no way to reach a human is not very trustworthy.

## Copy-and-Adapt Template for Small Businesses

Here is a starter you can customize. Replace the brackets, delete what does not apply, and never promise anything you cannot do.

```text
Editorial Policy of [Business Name]

Why we publish
We publish guides, tips, and updates to help [your customers] make informed decisions about [your topic]. This page explains how we create and maintain that content.

Who writes and reviews
Our content is written by [roles, e.g. our service team and owner] and reviewed by [role, e.g. a qualified specialist] before publishing. Author details are available on our [Authors] page.

How we research
We rely on [official documentation, government sources, supplier manuals, first-hand experience]. We attribute outside data and ideas to their original source.

How we use AI tools
We may use AI tools for [outlines, grammar checks, formatting]. A human editor reviews and verifies all content before it goes live. AI is never the sole author.

How we keep content current
Time-sensitive pages are reviewed every [3 months]. Other pages are reviewed every [12 months]. The "Last reviewed" date appears on this page.

Corrections
If you spot an error, email [address]. We reply within [2 business days] and fix confirmed mistakes within [7 business days]. Significant corrections are noted on the page.

Independence and disclosures
[State whether you use affiliate links, ads, or sponsorships, and that they do not influence recommendations.]

Contact
[Email] | [Phone] | [Contact page link]

Last reviewed: [Date]
```

## How to Publish Your Editorial Policy Page: Step by Step

1. **Write it in plain language.** Aim for 400 to 900 words. Short sentences, no legal fog.
2. **Use a clean URL.** Something like `/editorial-policy/` or `/press/editorial-policy.html` is easy to remember and easy to crawl.
3. **Add visible links.** Place a link in your site footer so it shows on every page. Link to it from your About page and, ideally, from a short note under each article.
4. **Connect it to author pages.** Each author should have a profile with real credentials, and the policy should link to them.
5. **Add a "Last reviewed" date.** A dated page signals that someone is paying attention.
6. **Add structured data.** Use Organization schema with the `publishingPrinciples` property pointing to your policy URL. It is a real Schema.org property made for exactly this purpose. Our [schema markup generator guide](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/26/schema-markup-generator-guide-why-it-matters.html) explains how to set it up.
7. **Keep your promises.** If you say you review quarterly, put a reminder in your calendar. A policy you ignore is worse than no policy.

Here is a simple example of the schema snippet:

```json
{
  "@context": "https://schema.org",
  "@type": "Organization",
  "name": "Your Business Name",
  "url": "https://www.example.com/",
  "publishingPrinciples": "https://www.example.com/editorial-policy/"
}
```

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Copy-pasting a big publisher's policy.** Their newsroom is not your business. Write what is true for you.
- **Promising things you cannot do.** "Every article is reviewed by three experts" is a risky line for a two-person team.
- **Hiding the page.** If it is not in the footer or linked from your About page, few people will find it.
- **Never updating it.** A policy dated three years ago with a broken contact link reads as neglect.
- **Being vague about AI.** Readers and platforms are becoming less tolerant of unclear AI use. Say what you actually do.
- **Treating it as a one-time SEO trick.** The page only works when your real process matches the words.

## Special Note for YMYL Businesses

If your content touches money, health, legal matters, or safety, Google calls these topics **YMYL (Your Money or Your Life)** and holds them to a higher standard. Think CA and CS firms, clinics, insurance agents, lawyers, loan advisors, and financial educators.

For these businesses, an editorial policy is close to essential. Make sure it states:

- Which qualified professional reviews sensitive content
- That content is educational and not personalized advice
- How you handle changes in laws, tax rules, or regulations
- How quickly outdated information gets corrected

Read more in our explainer on [what YMYL means in SEO](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/14/what-is-ymyl-in-seo.html).

## Key Takeaways

- An **editorial policy page** explains how your business researches, writes, reviews, updates, and corrects its content.
- It supports **E-E-A-T**, especially **Trust**, which Google describes as the most important part of the framework.
- It is a **supporting trust signal**, not a direct ranking factor and not a shortcut to position one.
- It helps **AI search engines and real readers** verify who you are and how your content is made.
- The best policies are **specific**: named roles, real timelines, honest AI disclosure, and a working corrections process.
- Link the page in your **footer**, connect it to **author pages**, and add **Organization schema** with `publishingPrinciples`.
- **YMYL businesses** such as finance, health, and legal firms benefit the most.
- Only publish promises you can **keep**. Review and date the page regularly.
