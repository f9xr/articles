You open your analytics on a Monday morning and your stomach drops. Organic traffic is down, enquiries have slowed, and the pages that used to bring in customers are sitting several positions lower. Then you see the headline everyone is talking about: Google has just finished a spam update.

Take a breath. A traffic drop during an update is stressful, but it is not a verdict on your business. It is a signal, and signals can be investigated.

Google's September 2026 spam update finished rolling out on October 8, 2026. That gives you a stable window to look at your data and work out what actually happened. This guide walks through how to confirm whether the update hit you, what to check on your site, how to fix real problems, and how long recovery realistically takes. No panic, just a clear plan.

## What Just Happened: The September 2026 Spam Update

Google released the update on September 24, 2026, and marked the rollout complete on October 8. The [Google Search Status Dashboard](https://status.search.google.com){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} is the page that tracks every confirmed update. Two details stand out. It was the longest spam update of 2026, and the fourth this year after March, June, and August. And Google has not said which spam tactics it targeted. Some SEOs say it felt similar to a core update. That is an observation, not something Google has stated.

## What Is a Google Spam Update?

A spam update is a notable improvement to Google's automated systems that detect content and practices breaking its [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}. Part of that detection runs through an AI-based system called SpamBrain.

This is different from a core update. A core update changes how Google judges quality across the board. A spam update focuses on content and tactics that violate the rules, although, as with any update, there can be collateral damage for honest sites.

### The Spam Policies That Matter Most

| Policy | What It Means in Plain English | Example on a Business Site |
|---|---|---|
| Scaled content abuse | Mass-producing pages mainly to rank, whether by AI, automation, or people | Hundreds of near-identical blog posts with no real expertise |
| Doorway abuse | Many pages targeting similar searches that lead to the same place | 200 "plumber in [area]" pages with swapped place names |
| Expired domain abuse | Buying an old domain to borrow its authority for unrelated content | A dead charity domain now selling loans |
| Site reputation abuse | Hosting low-value third-party content to ride on your site's reputation | A "coupons" or "reviews" section run by an outside party |
| Hidden text and link abuse | Content hidden from users but shown to search engines | White text on a white background stuffed with keywords |
| Scraped content | Copying other sites' content automatically | Auto-pulled product descriptions from competitors |
| User-generated spam | Spammy comments, profiles, or forum posts left unchecked | Casino links in blog comments |

That list is not complete. Read Google's own spam policies page for the full set.

## Google Spam Update Recovery Step 1: Confirm the Update Actually Caused Your Drop

Before changing anything, make sure you are fixing the right problem. A dip during an update window does not automatically mean the update caused it.

### Compare the Right Dates in Search Console

1. Open **Google Search Console** and go to the Performance report.
2. Compare the period **before September 24** with the period **after October 8**. Look at the days in between too.
3. Check clicks, impressions, and average position.
4. Filter by **page** and by **query** to see where the drop is concentrated.

### Rule Out Other Causes

| Possible Cause | How to Check |
|---|---|
| Manual action | Search Console, Manual Actions report. This is separate from an algorithm update. |
| Tracking problem | Compare Analytics against Search Console and real enquiries. |
| Technical issue | Look for noindex tags, robots.txt changes, broken redirects, or server errors. |
| Seasonality | Compare with the same weeks last year. |
| Other Google changes | Check whether a core update or another change overlaps your drop. |
| Local pack or Maps change | Check Google Business Profile insights separately from website traffic. |

If you find a manual action, the process is different. You fix the issue and file a reconsideration request. Algorithmic spam updates do not have a request button, so your site is reassessed automatically over time.

## Step 2: Find the Pattern in the Damage

Spam-related drops usually have a shape. Look for it.

- **Page-level drop:** Only certain pages or one section fell. Look at what those pages have in common, such as thin copy, template content, or third-party posts.
- **Site-wide drop:** Almost everything fell together. This suggests a broader problem with the site's overall content approach or trust signals.
- **Query-level drop:** Only certain keyword types fell, for example all "near me" or location pages.

Write down the pattern. It tells you where to spend your time first.

### A Simple Example

A local home services company created 150 city pages by changing the place name in the same paragraph. After an update, those pages lose rankings while the main service pages hold steady. The pattern points straight at doorway-style pages. The fix is not a technical trick, but replacing the copied pages with a smaller set of useful location pages.

## Step 3: Audit Your Site Against Google's Spam Policies

Now do an honest review. Ask these questions page by page. Our guide on [how to fact check and audit your website](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/10/04/how-to-fact-check-audit-your-website.html) is a good companion for this pass.

### Content Checklist

- Was this page written to help a person, or mainly to capture a search?
- Does it include real experience, local detail, or original information?
- Is it a near-copy of another page on your own site?
- Was it published in bulk with little human review?
- Is it factually correct and up to date?

### Site Checklist

- Do you host sections written by outsiders mainly for ranking benefit?
- Are there hidden links, hidden text, or odd redirects, which can be signs of a hack?
- Is your comment section or forum full of spam?
- Do you have many pages targeting almost identical keywords?
- Did you buy an old domain and change its topic completely?

Your results will usually fall into three groups: keep as is, improve, or remove.

## Step 4: Fix the Underlying Problems

### Improve Weak Pages Instead of Deleting Everything

Mass deletion in a panic can make things worse. Work in this order.

1. **Improve** pages that have a real purpose but thin content. Add original detail, real photos, pricing guidance, local examples, and answers to actual customer questions.
2. **Merge** pages that overlap heavily into one stronger page, and redirect the old URLs to it.
3. **Remove or noindex** pages that exist only to catch keywords and cannot be fixed honestly.
4. **Clean up** spam comments, hacked pages, and unwanted third-party sections.

### Fix AI and Template Content Carefully

AI tools are not banned. The issue is publishing large volumes with little checking or purpose. If you used automation heavily, have a human review each page, fix errors, add first-hand knowledge, and cut anything repetitive. Our guides on [checking whether your site looks like AI spam to Google](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/10/05/check-if-your-site-is-ai-spam-google.html) and [how to de AI slop your website](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/28/how-to-de-ai-slop-your-website.html) cover this in detail.

### Clean Up Your Links

Spam updates mostly concern on-site content, and Google usually puts "link" in the name when an update targets link spam. Even so, if you ever bought links or joined link schemes, stop now and review your backlink profile.

## Step 5: Rebuild Trust Signals

Fixing pages is half the job. The other half is showing both people and search systems that your business is real and reliable.

- Show real people. Add author names, bios, and credentials where they matter.
- Publish clear business details, including address, phone, hours, and a working contact page.
- Add policy pages. An editorial policy, correction policy, and privacy page signal accountability.
- Cite sources for facts, and keep statistics current.
- Collect customer reviews and respond to them.
- Keep your Google Business Profile consistent with your website.

---

## Step 6: Monitor, Wait, and Keep Improving

This is the hardest part. After you fix issues, Google does not instantly reverse a drop. Reports around this update say recovery from spam-related problems can take months, and some pages may recover only after systems reassess the site over time.

### A Realistic Google Spam Update Recovery Timeline

| Timeframe | What to Do | What to Expect |
|---|---|---|
| Week 1 | Diagnose, document, and rule out technical causes | Clarity about whether the update is the cause |
| Weeks 2 to 4 | Fix, merge, or remove problem pages | Little visible change yet |
| Weeks 4 to 12 | Improve content and trust signals, monitor Search Console | Gradual signs of stabilizing |
| 3 months and beyond | Keep publishing helpful content | Possible recovery, but not guaranteed |

This table is a planning guide, not a promise. Google does not publish a recovery schedule.

- Do not panic delete half your website.
- Do not chase quick fixes or guaranteed recovery services.

- Do not copy competitors who are also being hit.

## What This Means for Local Businesses

If you run a local business, remember that website rankings and Google Maps visibility are not the same thing. A spam update affects web search results. Your Google Business Profile, reviews, and local pack position follow different signals.

So check both. If your Maps visibility is stable but website traffic dropped, concentrate on your site content. If both fell, look for a shared cause such as inconsistent business details or review problems. ## Will This Affect AI Search Visibility Too?

Possibly, in an indirect way. AI search tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, and Perplexity use different data sources, and none of them publicly tie their answers to Google's spam updates. But the qualities that get a site demoted for spam, such as thin, copied, or untrustworthy content, are also the qualities that make a source less useful to cite.
