Think about the last time you used AI to write something for your website. A service page, a product description, one FAQ answer.

Did you check every claim on it against a real source, or did you read it once for tone and hit publish?

Most business owners did the second thing. That is not laziness. It is what happens when you are running a company instead of editing a magazine.

On October 1, 2026, Google updated the guidance page it keeps for using generative AI content on a website, and the new wording is harder to wave off than the old version. Google now says it is "critical" to manually fact-check and review all AI-generated content for accuracy and trustworthiness before publishing. It also says the review covers your titles, meta descriptions, structured data, and image alt text. Not just the paragraphs.

No penalty was announced, and nothing on that page claims Google can tell which pages you checked.

Translate that into money and it stops being theoretical. A false claim is not an abstract SEO problem. It is a refund request, a public review you will spend a year earning back, or a customer standing outside a storefront that closed an hour ago because your schema had the wrong hours.

This guide is the fact-check audit process, step by step, so you can run it on your own site without an editorial team.

## What Google Changed on October 1, 2026

The documentation page was last updated 2026-10-01 UTC. The relevant passage explains the mechanism first, then the instruction.

> Generative models don't retrieve facts, but predict a likely sequence of words based on their training data. Because of this, generative AI outputs may contain inaccuracies (also known as hallucinations). It is critical to manually factcheck and review all AI-generated content for accuracy and trustworthiness before publishing.

The word Google chose is "critical". That is not the language of a gentle suggestion.

The sentence most coverage skipped is the next one, and it is the one that changes your workload.

> This review also applies to metadata like `<title>` elements, meta description elements, structured data, and alternate texts for images, which can appear in Search results.

So the review boundary reaches past your blog posts into everything that renders directly in a search results page.

| What changed | Before | Now, October 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Fact-check instruction | Asked for accuracy, no explicit manual check | Calls manual fact-checking "critical" before publishing |
| Scope of review | Mostly implied to be body content | Named explicitly: titles, meta descriptions, structured data, alt text |
| Penalty language | None | Still none, the focus stays on accuracy and user value |
| Related risk | Low-quality content generally discouraged | Scaled content abuse still named directly |

[Search Engine Journal covered the change](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/google-fact-check-ai-content-before-publishing/591782/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} and confirmed the previous version carried no manual review instruction. [Search Engine Roundtable published the diff](https://www.seroundtable.com/google-updates-ai-content-guidelines-factcheck-review-42217.html){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}. We broke down the announcement and its timeline in [Google AI content guidance: fact-check by hand](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/10/02/google-ai-content-guidance-fact-check-update.html){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}, including what the update does not say.

The honest reading is narrow. Google is not asking you to choose between AI and no AI. It is stating who owns the responsibility for what your domain claims, and an AI tool is not a source of truth.

## What a Fact-Check Audit Actually Covers

A fact-check audit is not a content audit. You are not grading prose or keyword density. You are asking one question of every checkable statement on the page: is this true right now, and where is the proof?

### Body Copy

Every number, date, name, statistic, and quote has to trace back to something real. That includes claims that sound like harmless marketing, such as "trusted by thousands of customers" or "the leading provider in the region". Readers treat those as facts, and a reviewer will too.

### Titles and Meta Descriptions

These render in search results, so a wrong claim reaches people before they click. If your meta description promises a checklist and the page delivers a narrative, you have built a mismatch that costs you the click and the trust at the same time.

### Structured Data

Hours, pricing, star ratings, FAQ answers, and address details in your schema need to match what is true today, not what was true when the page was built. Schema decays quietly, because nothing warns you when a season ends or a price changes. Our [schema markup guide](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/26/schema-markup-generator-guide-why-it-matters.html){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} covers the structure side.

### Image Alt Text

Alt text should describe what is in the photo. AI-generated alt text often describes the category instead of the image, so every product photo becomes "blue running shoe". That is not a lie, but it is useless to a screen reader and thin as a ranking signal. Alt text is the most commonly skipped item on any audit list.

### Local and Business Details

Service areas, licensing, certifications, and pricing. An AI tool has zero knowledge of any of these. It will produce something plausible for all of them, and plausible is exactly what survives a quick read.

### Health, Legal, and Financial Claims

Anything that can change a real decision a customer makes needs a higher bar. This is the territory our explainer on [what YMYL means in SEO](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/14/what-is-ymyl-in-seo.html){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} covers, and the bar there is qualification, not just verification.

## How to Run a Fact-Check Audit, Step by Step

### Step 1: Build a Page Inventory

Write down every page on your site, with a flag beside anything a model touched, including title tags and meta descriptions an SEO tool generated. You cannot audit what you have not listed.

### Step 2: Flag Every Checkable Claim

Go page by page and highlight anything that can be proven true or false: numbers, dates, prices, names, certifications, statistics, and superlatives like "only", "best", or "guaranteed". Leave opinions alone. That is not what this pass is for.

Keep a lookup table beside you while you work, so you know instantly where each claim type gets verified.

| Claim you flagged | Where the proof lives |
|---|---|
| Statistic or study finding | The original paper or report, not the article that quoted it |
| Regulatory or legal requirement | The government or regulator's own page |
| Certification or license | The issuing body's registry |
| Price, discount, or offer | Your live pricing, checked against your checkout |
| Opening hours or service area | Your Google Business Profile and your own system |
| Star rating or review count | Your current review platform data |
| Named person, title, or credential | The person's own profile or the company page |
| "Trusted by", "leading", "best" | Nothing supports these unless you can count or cite it |

### Step 3: Verify Against Primary Sources

For each flagged claim, find the original. Government sites, official industry bodies, the original study, your own internal records, or a phone call to confirm a license number. A second AI tool agreeing with the first is not verification. It is two guesses pointing the same direction.

### Step 4: Audit Metadata and Schema as a Separate Pass

Pull every title tag, meta description, and schema block into one spreadsheet and review them as their own job. Run your markup through the [Rich Results Test](https://search.google.com/test/rich-results){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} to confirm it validates, then read the values inside it against what a customer would actually see.

### Step 5: Reconcile Local Details

Cross-check hours, address, phone, and service area against your Google Business Profile. A mismatch between your website and your listing is one of the most common and most avoidable trust problems on a small business site, and it is free to fix.

### Step 6: Get a Second Set of Eyes

Whoever wrote the page is the worst person to proofread it, because they read what they meant to say rather than what is actually there. Route anything touching health, legal, or financial claims to a second reviewer who can judge the substance, not just the grammar.

### Step 7: Date-Stamp What You Verified

Record the date of the last check on each page. Facts decay. Prices move, hours shift, certifications expire. A one-time sweep is a snapshot, not a system.

### Step 8: Add What Only You Know

Once the generic material is stripped out, replace it with something that could only come from your business: a real customer story, your actual process, your own photographs, your real numbers. This is also what separates useful content from the thin templated pages that Google's [scaled content abuse policy](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies#scaled-content){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} targets.

If the writing itself reads generic once the facts are fixed, that is a separate problem, and our guide on [how to de-AI-slop your website](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/28/how-to-de-ai-slop-your-website.html){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} handles it.

## Which Pages to Audit First

Not every page carries the same risk. Triage by what a wrong claim would cost you.

| Page type | Risk level | Audit priority |
|---|---|---|
| Health, legal, or financial advice pages | High | Audit first, every time |
| Service pages with pricing or licensing claims | High | Audit first |
| Local landing pages with hours, address, service area | Medium to High | Audit early |
| Product descriptions with specific feature claims | Medium | Audit on a rolling basis |
| Blog posts carrying statistics or external claims | Medium | Audit before your highest traffic pages |
| General informational or about pages | Low | Audit last |

If you only have a few hours this month, start at the top of that table and work down. The bottom of the table will not cost you a customer this week.

## Make the Audit Recurring

The audit that fails is the one that happens once. These are the habits that keep it alive.

- **Keep a log of where AI was used.** A simple spreadsheet naming the pages saves hours the next time you need to audit.
- **Put it in the calendar.** Monthly for small sites, quarterly at minimum. New pages and changed claims keep arriving.
- **Validate schema with a tool, not with your eyes.** A manual read misses structural errors every time.
- **Say how the page was made.** One line noting AI assistance and manual verification builds trust, and matches what Google's own guidance suggests.
- **Do not publish faster than you can verify.** One checked page beats ten unreviewed ones on rankings and on revenue.
- **Treat AI search engines like Google.** ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, and Perplexity read your pages and can repeat a wrong claim to a far larger audience than your own site ever served.

That last point is where the return has moved. Accurate, verifiable content is easier for answer engines to cite, and citation is what puts you in the answer.

Budget roughly an hour per page for a first pass, and about ten minutes per page for the recurring version once the claim list already exists. The first audit is expensive because you are building the list. Every audit after that is cheaper.

## Key Takeaways

- On October 1, 2026, Google updated its generative AI content guidance to call manual fact-checking "critical" before publishing AI-assisted content.
- The review scope covers titles, meta descriptions, structured data, and image alt text, not only body copy.
- Google has not introduced a penalty for AI-generated content itself. The risk it names directly is scaled content abuse, publishing many low-value pages without adding anything for users.
- Verify against primary sources. A second AI tool confirming the first is not verification.
- Prioritise high-risk pages first: health, legal, financial, and local service details.
- Record the date of the last check on every page, because facts and local details expire quietly.
- Accurate content is easier for both Google and AI answer engines to cite, which is where the visibility sits.

## Conclusion

The audit itself is not expensive. List the claims, check them at the source, review your metadata and schema separately, and date-stamp the result. Done consistently it runs about an hour per page, and it removes the most avoidable category of risk on a small business website.

Holding that standard across a growing site, alongside the technical and structural work that keeps it visible, is what consumes the real hours.
