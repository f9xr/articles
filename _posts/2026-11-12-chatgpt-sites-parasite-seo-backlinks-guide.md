Every few months an SEO shortcut circulates. This one is unusual because the shortcut is a product feature. OpenAI's ChatGPT Sites lets you describe a page in chat, watch ChatGPT build it, and publish it on OpenAI-managed hosting at a public URL.

The pitch for parasite SEO with ChatGPT is simple. Build a buyer-focused page, host it where the domain is already trusted, and link back to the site that pays your bills. You borrow authority, the page gets found faster, and your money page collects a link.

Only part of that is verified. ChatGPT Sites exists and can publish a public page. Whether that page gets indexed, whether the link counts for anything, and whether Google reads the arrangement as manipulation are three open questions. This guide separates what OpenAI and Google document from what SEO posts assume, then gives you a cheap test.

> **Quick answer:** ChatGPT Sites is OpenAI's website builder. You describe a page, it builds and hosts it, and you choose who can open it. The parasite SEO play is publishing a buyer-intent page there and linking it to your own service page. Treat it as a test. Check indexing and the link's rel attribute before assuming value, and keep the canonical version of the content on a site you own.

## What Parasite SEO With ChatGPT Sites Actually Means

Parasite SEO means publishing your content on a third party that already has authority, so the page can rank sooner than it would on a new domain. Guest posts, LinkedIn articles, Medium posts, and YouTube videos all count. You trade control for reach.

We covered the wider trade-offs in [what parasite SEO is and when a business should use it](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/17/what-is-parasite-seo-should-business-use-it.html), so here we'll stay with what is new.

ChatGPT Sites is OpenAI's built-in site builder. [Launch coverage from Playcode](https://playcode.io/blog/chatgpt-sites-explained){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} dates the public beta to July 9, 2026, inside ChatGPT Work, and OpenAI's [help center](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/20001339-creating-and-managing-chatgpt-sites){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} describes it as a way to create, preview, publish, and share interactive websites and lightweight apps. You ask for a website, or mention @Sites in a prompt, and it builds one.

Two details separate it from posting on Medium. First, you get a standalone site with its own address, not an article in a feed. Second, it can run interactive pieces such as calculators, quote forms, and dashboards. A local business could publish a "what will a redesign cost" calculator that qualifies leads, which does more work than a blog post.

There's a third detail that complicates the premise. Where custom domains are available, you can point a domain you already own at the Site. Do that and you are no longer borrowing anyone's authority, because the page sits on your domain. The parasite framing only applies while the page lives on a shared chatgpt.site address.

## What ChatGPT Sites Gives You, and What It Doesn't

Start with access, because it decides everything else. Sites runs in public beta on paid ChatGPT plans, and OpenAI's help center says plainly that Sites is not available on Free or Go.

| Plan | What you get today |
|------|--------------------|
| Plus or Pro | Hosting included, no fixed limit on the number of Sites, shared usage and storage limits apply |
| Business | Sites available, workspace settings and roles decide who can create and publish |
| Enterprise | Hosting charges currently waived, public publishing off until an admin enables it and grants role access |
| Free or Go | Not available |

Three limits matter more than the plan table.

| Limit | What it means |
|-------|---------------|
| Region | At launch, public publishing and the wider beta were held back in the EEA, Switzerland, and the UK. Confirm availability on your own account before you plan around it |
| Deployment | Every Sites deployment URL is a production URL. There is no staging link, so save a version and review it before you publish |
| Storage | Each Site's database holds up to 10 GB. File storage has no fixed per-Site limit, but the shared limits still apply |

Then the part the posts gloss over: authority. The case for parasite SEO is that you publish on a very strong domain. OpenAI's domain is strong. A chatgpt.site address is a different thing.

Ahrefs explains why in its guide to [Domain Rating](https://ahrefs.com/blog/domain-rating/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}. DR is a logarithmic score from 0 to 100 based on a site's backlink profile, and Google does not use it. More to the point, Ahrefs treats shared hosts such as wordpress.com, blogspot.com, squarespace.com, and tumblr.com as service root domains that do not pass their DR to the sites people create there, because those sub-sites belong to different owners. A shared chatgpt.site address fits that pattern.

So the inheritance is an assumption, not a documented behavior. Check the current DR for chatgpt.site in Ahrefs before you rely on it, and remember that a shared host's score does not automatically transfer to your page. If you want links you can measure, our guide to [getting backlinks in 2026](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/07/get-backlinks-2026-free-paid-ai-methods.html) covers methods with clearer value.

## The Google Policy That Decides Whether This Works

Google's spam policies include a section called the site reputation policy, the rule people mean when they say site reputation abuse. [The wording](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} is specific. It applies where third-party content is published on a host site mainly because of that host's already-established ranking signals, with the goal of ranking better than the content could on its own.

Then Google adds the line that changes how you should read everything else: having third-party content alone "isn't inconsistent with the site reputation policy." The acceptable examples include wire services, syndicated news, forums, and "columns, opinion pieces, articles, and other work of an editorial nature." The violation examples are pages dropped onto an unrelated host with no integration, published to rank off the host's reputation.

Read that carefully and the test isn't about the platform. It's about whether the page would deserve to exist on its own domain. A useful guide written by a named business, with original examples, looks a lot like the editorial example. A generic "best dentist in Austin" page mass-produced across ten hosts is the pattern the policy describes.

Which brings up the uncomfortable part. The tactic's name describes the prohibited intent. If your strategy document says "publish on a strong host so the page ranks faster than it could otherwise," you have written the policy's trigger sentence in your own words. Nobody outside Google can tell you how a specific page will be judged, and Google can demote or drop pages it considers in violation.

Our position: publish the page because it is useful, and use the host because it is convenient. If both reasons don't hold, skip it.

## How to Run the Play, Step by Step

Five steps for running parasite SEO with ChatGPT, in order. Total time is a few hours, and most of it goes into the content.

### Step 1: Pick one buyer question you can actually win

Bottom-of-funnel searches are where this kind of page does its job. The reader is already choosing, so the page has one task: help them decide.

| Pattern | Example | Page format |
|---------|---------|-------------|
| best + service + city | best orthodontist in Austin | Listicle with criteria |
| option A vs option B | Wix vs a custom site for a dental clinic | Comparison table |
| service + cost | website redesign cost for a small business | Pricing guide |
| alternatives to + tool | alternatives to Squarespace | Alternatives roundup |
| how to choose + service | how to choose a local SEO agency | Decision guide |

Those examples show the pattern, not keyword data. Check volume and difficulty in a tool before you commit. For more on matching the page to the query, read [decoding search intent](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/21/decoding-search-intent-what-customers-want.html).

One rule from our playbook: do not publish a head-to-head comparison you lose. Pick the angle where you have a real advantage.

### Step 2: Build the page around one decision

Ask ChatGPT for a website and describe the reader, the question, and the action you want. Include the word "website" or mention @Sites to trigger the builder. Then insist on structure:

- A single H1 that matches the search
- A short answer in the first paragraph
- A comparison table or checklist that makes the choice easier
- A small FAQ with two or three real questions
- One visible call to action

Keep it a content page. Sites can build dashboards and games, but every extra interactive layer is another thing that can hide your text and links from a crawler. Ask for semantic HTML, then check the raw source yourself in Step 5.

### Step 3: Add what only your business can say

This step separates a useful page from filler, and it also keeps you inside Google's policy. Include your process, your pricing approach, a case study with numbers you can prove, photos or screenshots from real work, and the limits of who the service is for.

Do not ask the model to invent statistics, reviews, or client names. Every number on the page should be one you can defend. Our guide to [writing listicles without spam](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/11/07/write-listicle-articles-without-spam-guide.html) shows how to keep list formats credible.

### Step 4: Link back with restraint

Link to the page on your own site that matches the reader's next step: a service page, a booking page, or a detailed article.

- Use descriptive anchor text, such as "local SEO packages for clinics"
- Link to the most relevant page, not the homepage by default
- Use two or three links, not a dozen
- Put each link where the reader would naturally click it

One technical note: Ahrefs counts only the first followed link from a domain toward DR. A second link from the same host adds little for authority, though it can still send traffic.

### Step 5: Publish, then verify before you believe

New Sites are private by default, so nothing is public until you change the audience. Choose Anyone on the internet where public publishing is enabled, save, and then run these checks.

1. Open the URL in a private window and confirm it loads without a ChatGPT login.
2. View the page source and confirm your H1, body text, and outbound link appear in the raw HTML, not only after scripts run.
3. Look for a noindex meta tag, and read the outbound link's rel attribute. If it says nofollow, the link is for traffic only.
4. Search the exact URL in Google after a few days. [URL Inspection](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9012289){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} only works on properties you own, and a chatgpt.site page is not one of yours, so use the Rich Results Test for a live render check instead.
5. Tag the outbound link with UTM parameters so referral clicks show up in your analytics.

If the page is indexed and the link is followed, you have the experiment the posts promised. If the link is nofollow, you have a distribution test. Both can be worth running, as long as you know which one you bought.

## A Test Plan That Tells You If It Worked

Keep it small. One topic, one page, one link, six weeks.

| Week | Action |
|------|--------|
| 1 | Pick three topics you can compete on, then choose the strongest |
| 1 | Publish the canonical version on your own site first |
| 1 | Build the ChatGPT Sites page as a rewritten, standalone version |
| 1 | Link to one relevant page, tag the link, and log the publish date |
| 2 to 6 | Check indexing weekly and watch referral clicks |
| 6 | Compare the results against the copy on your own domain and decide |

The log can be a spreadsheet with six columns: page URL, target keyword, publish date, indexed yes or no, link followed yes or no, and referral clicks plus leads. That is enough to make a decision without buying a new tool.

The decision rule matters more than the data. If the page is indexed and sends clicks, keep publishing. If it is not indexed after six weeks, stop, because the host is not passing what you hoped. If it is indexed and sends nothing, the topic or the page is the problem, not the platform.

For a wider set of measures, see [SEO metrics beyond rankings](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/19/seo-metrics-beyond-rankings.html).

## The Risks Worth Pricing In

Six failure modes, and what to do about each.

| Risk | Why it matters | What to do |
|------|----------------|------------|
| Google's site reputation policy | Publishing on a strong host mainly to borrow its ranking signals is the exact pattern the policy describes | Publish only pages that would stand on their own domain |
| Unconfirmed link value | The link may be nofollow, or the page may never be indexed | Check the rel attribute and indexing before counting on either |
| Platform control | OpenAI can remove or restrict Sites that risk violating its policies, and deleted sites cannot be restored | Keep a copy and host the canonical version yourself |
| Thin content | Generic pages rarely rank and can hurt trust in the brand | Add first-hand detail and proof |
| Legal and data duties | You are responsible for what you publish and for any personal data you collect | Do not collect data on these pages; send visitors to your own forms |
| Region limits | Public publishing was unavailable in the EEA, Switzerland, and the UK at launch | Confirm availability on your account first |

The risk we would underline is the last one on most lists: reliance. A page you do not control is a channel, not an asset. Every hour you spend on it is an hour not spent on the site that will still be yours when the product changes.

## Key Takeaways

- ChatGPT Sites can publish a public, standalone page on OpenAI-managed hosting, which makes it a candidate for parasite SEO experiments
- The borrowed-authority premise is unproven. Ahrefs treats shared hosts like wordpress.com and blogspot.com as service root domains that do not pass DR to sub-sites, and chatgpt.site fits that pattern
- Google's site reputation policy targets third-party content published on a host mainly to use the host's ranking signals, not all third-party content
- Check three things before believing the tactic works: the page is indexed, the link is followed, and the page sends clicks
- Publish the canonical version on your own site first, link once with descriptive anchor text, and tag the link for tracking
- Run it for six weeks with one page. Keep what gets indexed and clicked, and drop the rest

## Conclusion

Parasite SEO with ChatGPT Sites is a real publishing surface, and the play is cheap enough to test. It is not the shortcut the posts describe, because the two things that would make it one, a followed link from an inheriting domain and guaranteed indexing, are both undocumented.

So run it as an experiment with a defined end date. Pick a question you can answer better than the pages already ranking, put the canonical version on your own site, publish a distinct hosted copy, link once, and measure for six weeks. If it works, repeat it. If it doesn't, you have lost a few hours and learned something about where your traffic actually comes from.

That is a better trade than building a content plan on a metric nobody has measured.
