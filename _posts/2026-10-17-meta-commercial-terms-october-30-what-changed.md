If you run a Facebook Page, post on Instagram, or manage ads through Meta Business Suite, there is a deadline worth circling: **October 30, 2026**. That is the day Meta's updated Commercial Terms take effect. There is no "I agree" button. If you keep using Meta's business products after that date, you are treated as having accepted the new terms.

Most business owners never read these documents, and that is understandable. But this update has started a real debate, because it adds language about Meta crawling websites connected to your business and using what it finds to train its products. Meta says the intent is narrow. Legal experts say the wording could be read much more broadly.

There is a measurable difference between those two readings. On one side, your product pages stay product pages. On the other, every post, price list and premium article on your domain becomes training material, and the only compensation on offer is a liability cap that reviewers describe as unchanged. That gap is the reason this update is worth an hour of your time before the deadline.

Below we break down what is new, what is confirmed, what is still disputed, and what you can do this week to protect your website and your marketing. It is written for shop owners and startups, not just publishers.

*Quick note: this article is for general information and is not legal advice. Details were current as of October 17, 2026, and the situation may change before the effective date.*

## What Is New in Meta's Commercial Terms?

The previous version of these terms dates from March 6, 2024. The new version carries over a lot of the old wording, but adds several things that matter to small businesses. Let us go through them one at a time. You can read the full text in [Meta's Commercial Terms preview](https://www.facebook.com/legal/commercial_terms_preview).

### 1. A new website crawling and AI training clause

This is the headline change. In the section now titled "Licences and rights you provide Meta," a new paragraph says you authorize Meta, or anyone acting for it, to use automated software to reach domains, pages or URLs associated with your products or services. The list of automated tools is broad: scrapers, bots, crawlers and user agents. The paragraph also allows Meta to create test credentials, to access and cache content, and to pull updates periodically.

Meta may then use that content to provide, develop, improve, research and train its products, services and features.

A few things stand out:

- The old version had no crawling paragraph at all. It only covered content you posted or uploaded to Meta's own products.
- The key phrase is "associated with your products or services." The terms do not define it. A retailer might read that as product pages. A publisher might read it as the whole site.
- A second part of the clause also covers URLs made available through your or your users' use of Meta products, which is wider still.
- The word "train" appears in the list of permitted purposes.

### 2. A brand new "AI features" section

Meta has added a section on AI features. In plain terms, it says Meta may offer features powered by AI, that those features can make mistakes, and that you are solely responsible for the risks of those features and any content they produce. It also says you must not remove or alter watermarks or authentication metadata that AI adds to content.

For advertisers, this ties into Meta's new Self-Serve Ad Terms, announced alongside the Commercial Terms on October 8. According to summaries from agencies that reviewed them, advertisers are responsible for changes made by automated processes and Meta's AI, and Meta can run tests on accounts that may affect pricing, targeting, delivery and creative.

### 3. A stronger "as is" disclaimer and an unchanged liability cap

The warranty section now states more firmly that Meta's products are provided "as is" and disclaims implied warranties. The liability cap is reported as unchanged: the greater of $100 or what you paid Meta in the previous twelve months. For a small business, that is a low ceiling compared with what a disrupted ad account or a lost Page can cost. Use that ratio as your risk baseline: you carry the downside, Meta caps its own at roughly $100.

### 4. A wider indemnity

The old clause covered claims brought by third parties. The new heading reads "Indemnification" and extends to claims brought by you or anyone else. Lawyers who reviewed the text flagged this as a quiet but meaningful shift toward businesses carrying more risk.

### 5. Other terms folded in

The new terms incorporate several supplemental agreements by reference, including the Self-Serve Ad Terms, Business Tools Terms (covering tools such as Meta Pixel and Conversions API), the Seller Agreement, and terms for Meta's business agents and AI business assistant. There is also a clause reserving Meta's right to modify, limit, suspend or discontinue commercial products and features at any time without prior notice or liability.

### 6. A broader definition of "business use"

The definition now lists more activities, including marketing messages, listing products, creating or using a product catalog, and using business tools such as Ads Manager or Meta Business Suite. In other words, if you do anything commercial on Meta, assume you are covered.

## What Meta Says, and What Critics Say

This part matters, because the story is not one sided.

**Meta's position.** After Nieman Lab [published its report](https://www.niemanlab.org/2026/10/metas-new-commercial-terms-will-allow-the-scraping-of-news-sites-legal-experts-say/) on October 15, Meta's public affairs team said the crawling language had previously been scattered across other terms and that the update consolidates it in one place. A spokesperson also said content owners can still manage access through robots.txt. On October 16, Meta communications lead Andy Stone publicly called reports about scraping news sites false. He said nothing changes about how Meta uses web crawlers, and that the update is meant to let Meta pull accurate information about products for sale on third party websites for ads and content recommendations.

**The critics' position.** Three legal experts told Nieman Lab that the clause, as written, could support much more than product data. Their views, in summary:

- One media and IP attorney noted the material could be worth a great deal in a licensing market and advised publishers to assume the clause would be enforceable.
- A Cornell Law professor read it as clear permission to train AI on site content, and said publishers must either trust Meta not to use the full scope or stop using business accounts.
- A University of Wisconsin law professor compared it to "tying," where access to one product depends on accepting another permission, similar to complaints about Google's AI search.

The experts also raised the possibility that Meta could argue its contract overrides other blocking instructions. The terms do not mention robots.txt at all, so they neither carve it out nor override it.

**Where that leaves you.** There is a gap between what Meta says it intends and what the contract text allows. Intent can change. The contract is what applies after October 30. PPC Land has a [detailed section by section analysis](https://ppc.land/metas-new-terms-let-it-crawl-business-websites-to-train-ai-from-oct-30/) that is worth reading if you want the full wording. That gap is the practical reason to take this seriously, even if you believe Meta will behave as described.

*Why a small business owner gave the story its start:* reports say a small publisher who spotted the notice in his Meta Business Suite dashboard tipped off Nieman Lab. These notices are easy to miss, which is exactly why it pays to know where they appear.

## Why This Matters Beyond News Publishers

It is easy to read the headlines and think this is a publisher problem. It is not. The clause refers to websites associated with any business using Meta's products for a commercial purpose. That includes:

- A local restaurant with a menu page and a Facebook Page
- An online store running Instagram and Facebook ads
- A clinic, salon or coaching business with a booking site
- A startup that uses Meta Pixel and Conversions API on its domain
- A freelancer who manages client pages from Meta Business Suite

If your website content, product descriptions, pricing, photos or blog posts took effort to create, you have a stake in how they are used.

The timing also connects to bigger shifts. Meta has been building AI shopping and assistant features. Reporting says product browsing inside Meta ads switches on for existing ads on October 28, so a tap on an ad may show product details inside the app before the shopper reaches your site. Meta's Muse assistant also expanded to small businesses in late September. Our own coverage of the retail side is here: [Amazon blocks Meta Muse AI assistant](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/29/amazon-blocks-meta-muse-ai-assistant.html).

Crawler traffic is growing too. Cloudflare data reported by PPC Land showed Meta's share of AI crawling traffic rising from 0.9% to 7.5% between July 2024 and July 2025. A separate analysis reported that roughly one in five top websites block Meta's main crawler in robots.txt. That figure comes from a single source, so treat it as a rough indicator rather than a benchmark.

## What Should Business Owners Do Before October 30?

Here is a practical checklist. You do not need to do everything. Pick what fits your business. Set aside an afternoon, and treat it as risk reduction rather than a project.

### Step 1: Find out who controls your Meta business accounts

In many small businesses, the person who set up the Facebook Page left months ago, or an agency still has admin access. Open Meta Business Suite and list every admin, every connected Page, Instagram account, ad account and catalog. Decide who in your company is authorized to accept terms on your behalf.

### Step 2: List every website and domain linked to your Meta presence

Check these places:

- The website field on your Facebook Page and Instagram profile
- Link in bio tools and landing pages
- Product catalogs and commerce settings
- Domains verified in Business Manager
- Domains where Meta Pixel or Conversions API is installed
- Pages you regularly share in posts and ads

This list tells you what could fall under "associated with your products or services."

### Step 3: Decide what content you are comfortable sharing

Not everything on a website has the same value. Product pages meant to be seen widely are different from premium articles, member only content, proprietary research, pricing for private clients or course materials. Mark what is sensitive and decide how to protect it.

### Step 4: Review and update your robots.txt

Robots.txt is the standard way to tell crawlers what they may access. Meta's [crawler documentation](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/sharing/webmasters/web-crawlers) describes several crawlers with different roles:

| Crawler | Reported purpose | What to consider |
|---|---|---|
| Meta-ExternalAgent | Collects public pages for AI training and for indexing in Meta products, reportedly under one token | Because one token covers both uses, you cannot allow indexing while refusing training with this agent |
| Meta-WebIndexer | Builds the index used for Meta AI answers | Blocking may reduce how your content appears in Meta AI answers |
| FacebookExternalHit | Fetches a page only when someone shares a link, to build the preview card | Blocking it can break link previews on Facebook and Instagram, so most businesses should leave it alone |

An example of a rule that blocks Meta's main AI crawler looks like this:

```text
User-agent: meta-externalagent
Disallow: /
```

Before you copy anything, double check the current crawler names in Meta's documentation, since they can change. Keep two caveats in mind as well. Robots.txt is voluntary, and it works only if the crawler respects it. Legal experts warned that a signed contract could be argued to take priority over a technical signal. Meta says that is not its intent, but the text does not say so. The technical standard behind robots.txt, [RFC 9309](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9309), explains those limits.

For a deeper walkthrough, read our guide on [how to block AI crawlers on your site](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/04/block-ai-crawlers-on-your-site.html) and our explainer on [essential txt files for SEO, AEO and GEO](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/08/essential-txt-files-seo-aeo-geo-2026.html).

### Step 5: Add a second layer if the content is valuable

If your content is truly sensitive, do not rely on robots.txt alone. Options include:

- Firewall or CDN bot rules (for example through Cloudflare) that challenge or block unwanted bots
- Putting premium content behind real authentication
- Rate limiting and monitoring server logs for unusual crawler activity
- Reviewing signup flows, since the terms mention "test credentials," so make sure registration or trial forms do not expose paid content freely

Be careful not to block search engines such as Googlebot or Bingbot by accident. Your SEO still depends on them.

### Step 6: Review your ad account settings and AI features

If you run Meta ads, look at the automation and AI features enabled on your account. Check how creative generation, placements and audience expansion are set. Since the new ad terms make advertisers responsible for AI driven changes, review ads regularly instead of assuming everything is fine. Do not remove AI labels or watermarks from generated assets.

### Step 7: Weigh the trade off honestly

Realistically, your choices are:

| Option | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| Keep using Meta and accept the terms | Keep your audience, ads and tools | You accept the crawling and AI clause as written |
| Stay, but harden your website | Keep reach while limiting crawler access | Requires technical setup and ongoing monitoring |
| Reduce or stop business use of Meta | Full control over your content | You lose reach, ads and customer conversations on the platform |

For most local businesses, Meta still brings customers who buy. A reasonable approach is to stay, tighten your technical setup, and diversify so that you are not dependent on one platform. See our guide on [how AI picks local businesses](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/16/how-ai-picks-local-businesses-2026.html) and our look at [structuring local business data for Meta AI indexers](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/19/structuring-local-business-data-meta-ai-indexers.html).

### Step 8: Talk to a lawyer if the stakes are high

If your business sells premium content, owns valuable research, or has contractual obligations to clients about data and content use, ask a qualified lawyer to read the terms. This article cannot tell you whether the clause is enforceable in your situation. Even the experts who spoke to reporters said it is unclear how a court would treat it.

### Step 9: Document today's state of your site

Take dated screenshots of your robots.txt, your terms page and your key content. Keep a note of when you reviewed Meta's terms. If a dispute ever comes up, records help.

Meta's October 30 update is not the end of the world for small businesses, but it is a clear signal. Platform terms are changing quickly as AI becomes central to how products and content are discovered, and the fine print now reaches past the platform into your own website.

Know who controls your accounts, list the domains tied to them, tighten your robots.txt and bot rules, protect your most valuable content, and keep building channels you own.
