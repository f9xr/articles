You spent months building the site. A freelancer, or a chat window on a busy Sunday night, helped you fill it with service pages, city pages, and a blog.

Everything looked fine. Then one Monday you opened Search Console and the traffic line was pointing down.

A technical glitch? A slow season? Or did Google quietly decide your site looks like AI spam?

That question is a lot more common than it was a year ago. Google began its [September 2026 spam update on September 24](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/10/03/google-september-2026-spam-update-guide.html){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}, the fourth spam update of the year. A week later, on October 1, Google revised its guidance on generative AI content and told site owners to fact-check AI output by hand before publishing.

You do not need an SEO degree to check your own site. This guide gives you a nine-point self-audit, a scorecard that turns the answers into a risk level, and a 30-day plan for fixing whatever it finds. It is written for startups, local businesses, and anyone who used AI to move faster and now wants to be sure it was done safely.

> **Short version:** Google does not penalize a site for using AI. It targets pages made at scale, mainly to manipulate rankings, that give people little value. To check yourself, ask five questions. Would each page deserve to exist without AI? Are the facts verified? Do real people and real experience show up? Are many pages near copies of each other? Does Search Console show unexplained indexing or traffic problems? Mostly good answers mean you are in decent shape.

## What Google Actually Means by "AI Spam"

Most of the panic comes from a misunderstanding, and it is worth clearing up before you audit anything.

Google has never had a rule that says AI content is spam. What it has is a spam policy called [scaled content abuse](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}, introduced in 2024. In plain terms, it covers producing large numbers of pages mainly to manipulate search rankings without adding value for users. The policy looks at purpose and value, not at the tool you used.

A thousand weak pages written by humans in a content mill are the same policy violation as a thousand weak pages from a chatbot.

The generative AI guidance adds a second point. AI models predict likely words, they do not look up facts, so their output can contain errors. Google's [October 1 update](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/using-gen-ai-content){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} says it is critical to manually fact-check and review AI-generated content before publishing, and that the review covers titles, meta descriptions, structured data, and image alt text as well as body copy.

So when people say "AI spam," they usually mean one of two things.

| The pattern | What it looks like in practice |
|---|---|
| Volume without value | Many near-identical or low-effort pages |
| Confident nonsense | Unchecked AI text with wrong facts, invented statistics, or filler |

Read them side by side and the difference is obvious.

| Healthy AI-assisted content | The pattern that gets sites in trouble |
|---|---|
| AI helps with an outline, then a person adds real experience | Raw AI output published without review |
| Each page answers a distinct question | Hundreds of pages that differ only by city or keyword |
| Facts, prices, and hours checked against real records | Invented statistics, fake quotes, wrong business details |
| Named authors with real credentials | No author, no accountability, no first-hand detail |
| Fewer pages, each one useful | Publishing volume as the main goal |

## The 9-Point Self-Audit: Is Your Site AI Spam?

Work through these in order and keep a simple spreadsheet. Mark each check pass, warning, or fail.

### 1. The "Would This Exist Without AI?" Test

Pick ten pages at random and ask one question of each. If AI tools did not exist, would someone have bothered to write this page?

If the honest answer is "no, it only exists because it was cheap to make," that is a warning sign. A page that exists because customers keep asking the question is healthy. A page that exists to catch one more keyword variation is not.

**Pass:** most pages have a clear reason to exist. **Fail:** many pages exist only to fill a keyword list.

### 2. Page Count Versus Real Value

Open your sitemap and count the indexable pages. Then open Search Console, go to Performance, set the date range to the last 12 months, and look at pages by clicks.

If you have 300 pages and 250 earned almost nothing, that is not automatically spam, but it deserves a hard look. Ask what those pages are for. Our guide on [reviving dead content with systematic audits](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/20/reviving-dead-content-systematic-audits.html){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} shows how to decide which pages to improve, merge, or retire.

**Pass:** most pages earn impressions or serve a clear business purpose. **Fail:** a large share of pages do nothing and say nothing new.

### 3. Near-Duplicate Location and Service Pages

This is the most common problem we see on local business sites. It looks like "Plumber in Area A," "Plumber in Area B," and 40 more, with the same paragraphs and only the place name swapped.

Google groups the broader pattern under doorway abuse. The test takes about ten seconds: open two location pages side by side. If swapping the area name makes them identical, they are too similar.

A fair location page carries local detail. Projects you completed there, nearby landmarks, local reviews, staff who serve that area, questions locals actually ask.

**Pass:** each location page has unique, specific detail. **Fail:** the pages are the same text with find and replace.

### 4. Fact-Check Everything, Including the Hidden Parts

AI tools cannot know your opening hours, service area, licenses, or pricing. They will still write something that sounds right. That is exactly what slips through a quick read.

Following Google's October guidance, check these separately.

| Element | What to verify |
|---|---|
| Body copy | Every statistic, date, name, quote, and claim |
| Title tags and meta descriptions | That they honestly describe the page |
| Structured data (schema) | Hours, address, phone, prices, and FAQ answers match what visitors see |
| Image alt text | Describes the actual image, not a keyword list |
| Outbound links | They work and support the claim |
| Health, legal, or money claims | Reviewed by someone qualified to review them |

Use the [Google Rich Results Test](https://search.google.com/test/rich-results){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} for schema. For the full workflow, read our guide on [how to fact-check audit your website](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/10/04/how-to-fact-check-audit-your-website.html){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}.

**Pass:** you can point to a source for every checkable claim. **Fail:** you cannot say where a number came from.

### 5. Real People, Real Accountability

Spam sites are usually anonymous. Trustworthy sites have names, faces, and a way to reach them.

Look for named authors with short honest bios, a real About page with your story and address, contact details that match your Google Business Profile, policies for privacy and refunds, and genuine customer reviews.

Our [E-E-A-T checklist](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/04/google-e-e-a-t-checklist-every-seo-should-bookmark.html){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} and the guide on [how to increase your website trust score](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/15/how-to-increase-website-trust-score.html){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} go deeper on each of these.

**Pass:** a visitor can tell who runs the site and how to reach them. **Fail:** the site could belong to anyone.

### 6. Do You Add Something Only You Can Add?

This is the heart of it. If your page says what every other page on the topic already says, there is no reason for Google, ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, or Perplexity to prefer yours. Google's [people-first content guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} puts it in similar terms.

Ways to add what a competitor cannot copy:

- Photos from your actual jobs, shop, or team
- Prices or timelines from your own experience
- Answers to questions your customers really ask on the phone
- Case studies with real, permitted detail
- Your own opinion, including what you would not recommend

**Pass:** each key page contains at least one thing a competitor could not copy. **Fail:** your pages could be swapped with a competitor's and nobody would notice.

### 7. The AI Slop Language Check

Read your homepage intro out loud. Does it sound like a person talking, or like a brochure written by a committee?

Common signs of unedited AI text:

- Openings like "In today's fast-paced digital world"
- Every paragraph the same length and rhythm
- Vague claims like "we offer comprehensive solutions tailored to your needs" with no specifics
- Repeated phrases across many pages
- Lists of three everywhere

Editing for voice is not cosmetic. Generic text is a quality problem in its own right. Our walkthrough on [how to de-AI-slop your website](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/28/how-to-de-ai-slop-your-website.html){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} shows how to fix this page by page.

**Pass:** pages sound like your business. **Fail:** pages could belong to any business in any city.

### 8. Borrowed Reputation and Risky Shortcuts

Google's spam policies cover more than AI, so scan for these while you are in there.

| Risk | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Expired domain abuse | A bought domain whose old topic has nothing to do with your business |
| Site reputation abuse | Unrelated third-party content hosted on your site with little oversight |
| Paid or swapped links | Link packages, directories bought in bulk, link exchange schemes |
| Hidden text and links | Text matching the background color, or tiny off-screen text |
| Cloaking and sneaky redirects | Googlebot and visitors seeing different things |
| Hacked content | Strange pages, or pharmacy and casino links you never created |
| Unmoderated user content | Spam in comments and forums |

For the grey areas, see our explainer on [what parasite SEO is and whether a business should use it](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/17/what-is-parasite-seo-should-business-use-it.html){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}.

**Pass:** none of these apply. **Fail:** any of these apply and you have not dealt with them.

### 9. What Google's Own Tools Are Telling You

Finally, let Google speak for itself. In Search Console:

1. **Manual actions report.** You want "No issues detected." A spam update is algorithmic, so a manual action would be a separate matter.
2. **Pages report.** Watch for large numbers of "Crawled, currently not indexed" or "Discovered, currently not indexed." That can mean Google is not impressed with those pages, though technical issues cause it too.
3. **Performance report.** Compare full weeks before and after September 24. If the drop started earlier, the spam update is probably not the cause.

Rule out the boring causes first: a broken analytics tag, a noindex tag added by accident, or a robots.txt change. Our [monthly website audit checklist](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/03/monthly-website-audit-checklist.html){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} walks through these.

**Pass:** no manual action, healthy indexing, no unexplained drop. **Fail:** large indexing gaps, or a drop that lines up with a spam update and a known weakness from the checks above.

## Your Quick Risk Scorecard

Count how many of the nine checks came out as a fail. This is an F9XR rule of thumb, not a Google metric, but it tells you where to start.

| Fails | What it suggests | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 1 | Low risk | Keep monitoring monthly and keep adding original content |
| 2 to 4 | Some real weaknesses | Fix the failed items over the next 30 days |
| 5 or more | High risk | Pause new publishing, audit every page, and consider expert help |

## Should You Trust AI Detector Tools?

Plenty of business owners paste a page into a detector, see "80% AI," and panic.

Detector tools are estimates, nothing more. Independent audits of commercial detectors have found false-positive rates above 60% for non-native English writers, and overall misclassification rates above 70% on some open-source tools. They also miss lightly edited AI text. Nothing in Google's published guidance says detector scores affect rankings.

Use a detector at most as a rough smell test. The better test is the checklist above: is it true, is it useful, is it yours?

## What to Do If You Find Problems

Resist the urge to delete half your site in a panic, especially during a rollout. Rankings can swing and sometimes reverse while an update is still running.

A calm 30-day plan:

| When | Action |
|---|---|
| Days 1 to 3 | Log your traffic data, check Search Console, rule out technical causes |
| Days 4 to 10 | Run the nine-point audit and list every weak page |
| Days 11 to 20 | For each weak page, decide: improve, merge and redirect, or remove |
| Days 21 to 30 | Add real experience, author detail, verified facts, and schema checks to your best pages |
| Ongoing | Run a monthly audit and publish fewer, better pages |

For every weak page, ask in this order.

1. **Can it be improved?** Add original detail and verified facts.
2. **Can it be merged?** Combine thin pages into one strong page and use a 301 redirect.
3. **Should it go?** If it has no reason to exist, remove it properly.

Recovery is rarely instant. Google's systems need time to reassess a site after you improve it, so keep changes steady and keep your log updated.

### An Illustrative Example

*This is a hypothetical scenario to show how the process works, not a client result.*

A home services company serving 40 neighborhoods has a page for each one. Thirty of them are the same 300 words with a different area name. Traffic is flat, and Search Console shows many of those pages as crawled but not indexed.

The fix is not to delete everything. The owner keeps the 10 areas where the company has real jobs, rewrites each page with local project photos, a customer quote, and actual service details, then merges the other 30 into three regional pages. Fewer pages, each one genuinely different, and a site that can be defended page by page.

## What This Means for ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, and Perplexity

Spam updates are a Google Search matter, and there is no guarantee they affect AI assistants directly. The habits that keep you safe in Google tend to help elsewhere anyway.

Answer engines pull live web content and need pages they can trust and quote. Content that is accurate, clearly written, dated, and attributed to a named source is easier to rely on than anonymous filler. Consistent business details across your website, Google Business Profile, and directories also help these systems work out who you are.

## Key Takeaways

- **Google does not penalize AI use itself.** It targets scaled content abuse, meaning many pages made mainly to manipulate rankings with little value for users.
- **The September 2026 spam update began September 24.** It is the fourth spam update of the year, and Google has not named a specific target.
- **Google's October 1 guidance says to fact-check AI content by hand,** including titles, meta descriptions, structured data, and image alt text.
- **Ask whether each page deserves to exist without AI.** A page that only exists because it was cheap to produce is a risk.
- **Near-duplicate location pages are the most common local business problem.** Rewrite them with real local detail or merge them.
- **Real authors, real experience, and original detail** are the strongest protection available.
- **AI detector scores are not a ranking factor you can rely on.** Judge pages on accuracy and usefulness instead.
- **Check Search Console before blaming Google:** manual actions, indexing status, and the dates before and after the update.
- **Fix by improving, merging, or removing,** then give recovery time.

## Conclusion

Nobody can promise that a site will never be hit by a spam update. What you can do is stop guessing. Check whether your pages deserve to exist, whether the facts are true, whether real people stand behind them, and whether Google's own tools show anything odd.

Most honest businesses find a handful of fixable weak spots and a lot of pages that are fine.

The sites that struggle are the ones built on shortcuts: bulk pages, borrowed reputation, unchecked text. The sites that hold up publish less, verify more, and sound like the business they actually are.
