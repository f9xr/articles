Two years ago, YMYL felt like a term for hospitals, banks, and law firms. Not a marketing blog run by a small agency.

Then we read our own archive a little more honestly. F9XR Articles publishes advice on ad budgets, hosting costs, domain purchases, and recovering traffic after a spam penalty. Readers make spending decisions partly on what we write. Under Google's Search Quality Rater Guidelines, that is enough to land a site in the lower half of YMYL scrutiny, even when nobody is handing out medical or investment advice.

So we audited ourselves. This is the write-up. Why a marketing site still qualifies as YMYL, the five-pass method we used, what we found on our own pages, and the scoring sheet you can run on your site this week.

If the term is new to you, start with our explainer on [what YMYL means in SEO](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/14/what-is-ymyl-in-seo.html). Everything below builds on it.

## YMYL Is Broader Than It Sounds

YMYL stands for Your Money or Your Life. Google uses the label for pages where inaccurate information could hurt someone's health, finances, safety, or a major life decision.

Most owners hear that and picture WebMD or a brokerage blog. The guideline is not about the label on your business. It is about the potential for harm. If a reader can lose money because your page was wrong, you are in scope.

That test catches more sites than people expect:

- A photographer whose page explains deposit contracts
- A SaaS blog showing founders how to budget for Google Ads
- A local agency advising on hosting, domains, or paid indexing

We are the last example. Our content is educational marketing, not medical advice. But a post about hosting choice, or a [paid indexing service](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/30/how-paid-indexing-services-work-tech-truth-risks.html), can steer a real purchase. That is a money decision. So we are in the YMYL conversation whether we like it or not.

## Why We Audited Our Own Site

Two things pushed us off the fence.

The first was growth. In roughly four weeks our archive went from 71 posts to 113. Volume changes your risk profile. Google's September 2026 spam update put extra weight on scaled content, and a fast-growing site with a mostly shared byline can start to look like the pattern that update targets.

The second was a promise. Our [Editorial Policy](https://f9xr.org/articles/press/editorial-policy.html) says every article is human-reviewed and that time-sensitive posts are refreshed every 90 days. Promises are easy to write and easy to leave unverified. We wanted to know whether the page matched the archive.

An audit is how you find that out. Not a vibe check. A count.

---

## The Five-Pass Audit Method

We did not buy a tool. We read our own repository, counted things, and wrote down every gap with a severity and a fix. YMYL is mostly an evidence question: can a rater verify your claims from what is visible on the page? So we treated evidence as the whole job.

**Pass 1, inventory every page:** we listed all 113 posts with three fields each, author, publish date, and modified date. A spreadsheet or a simple search across the folder is enough.

**Pass 2, classify by risk:** we tagged each post low, medium, or high, based on one question. Could wrong advice here cost a reader money? A post about the history of SEO is low. A post comparing hosting providers is high.

**Pass 3, score E-E-A-T signals:** for every high-risk post we checked five things, a named author, a credential or profile link, cited sources, a review or update date, and a clear disclaimer.

**Pass 4, check monetization:** we reviewed the ads and [affiliate links](https://f9xr.org/articles/press/affiliate-disclosure.html) for labeling, density, and placement. Raters notice aggressive or misleading commercial content, especially on advice pages.

**Pass 5, verify structured data and trust pages:** we checked the BlogPosting and author schema, the publisher entity, and the full [policy library](https://f9xr.org/articles/press/transparency.html) behind the site.

The output looked like any audit report: findings, evidence, priority, fix. That structure matters, because it turns a vague worry into three or four concrete tasks.

## What We Found on Our Own Site

Here is the honest version. Some of it was already strong. Some of it was not.

| Signal | State at the last audit | State now | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Modified date on posts | 56 of 71 | 112 of 113 | Fixed |
| Named human author | Minority of posts | About 41 percent | Improving |
| "Reviewed by" field | 0 | 0 | Open |
| Visible review date | None | None | Open |
| Physical address or legal entity | Not published | Not published | Open |
| Affiliate disclosure page | None | Published | Fixed |
| FAQ block on posts | Common | All 113 posts | Strong |
| Site-level Organization schema | Per-post only | Per-post only | Open |

The modified date was the easy fix, and it is now almost complete. The named-author share is climbing, but a majority of posts still carry the team byline, including several commercial posts. And the two hardest gaps are still open: no reviewer is named on any post, and the site does not publish a registered address.

That is the point of doing this in public. The audit did not make the site look bad. It made the next three moves obvious, and it is the same three moves most small sites should make first. We wrote up the fuller version in our [real audit findings](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/10/01/monthly-website-audit-real-findings.html) post as well.

## The Rubric You Can Copy

You do not need our specific numbers. You need a checklist that forces an honest answer on every risky page. This is the one we used. Score each row 0, 1, or 2.

| Signal | Question to answer | Score 2 looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Named author | Is there a real human attached to this page? | Full name and profile link |
| Credentials | Why is that person qualified on this topic? | Education or experience on their profile |
| Sources | Are claims backed by primary sources? | Links to official docs or data |
| Review trail | Was a second person involved? | "Reviewed by" with a name and date |
| Update date | When was this last checked? | A visible, recent review date |
| Disclaimer | Is the advice framed as educational? | Clear note that it is not professional advice |
| Ownership | Can a reader tell who runs the site? | About page plus a verifiable location |
| Monetization | Are ads and links labeled and calm? | Labeled ads, marked affiliate links |

A page that scores 16 is in good shape. A high-risk page that scores under 10 is where you start. The rubric is not a ranking formula. It is a way to make the invisible visible, the same idea behind our [E-E-A-T checklist](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/04/google-e-e-a-t-checklist-every-seo-should-bookmark.html).

## How to Run This Audit on Your Site

Here is the short version you can do without any paid tools.

**1. List your money pages:** pull every page that gives advice someone could act on. Budgeting, vendors, contracts, health, safety, taxes. This is your high-risk set.

**2. Read one as a stranger:** open your best page and answer two questions. Who wrote this, and why should I believe them? If you have to scroll or guess, the page fails the first test a rater runs.

**3. Score the five core signals:** author, credentials, sources, review date, disclaimer. Use the rubric above. Do not score your whole site. Score the five highest-risk pages first.

**4. Fix the three cheapest wins:** most sites can add a named author box, a review date, and a proper About and contact page in a week. Those three cover a lot of ground, and they support one another. An author name means nothing if the profile page is empty. The trust basics are covered in our guide to [raising your website trust score](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/15/how-to-increase-website-trust-score.html).

**5. Check the commercial layer:** label every ad and affiliate link. Keep density low. Never place an ad between a reader and the answer they came for. Our own [summary of a Google quality update](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/10/02/google-ai-content-guidance-fact-check-update.html) touched on this from the content side.

**6. Put it on the calendar:** YMYL pages go stale. Laws change, prices change, platforms change. Tie your review schedule to the one in your editorial policy so the promise and the practice match.

**7. Add the schema last:** point an Organization block at your editorial policy with the `publishingPrinciples` property, and keep your Article schema accurate. Our [schema markup guide](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/26/schema-markup-generator-guide-why-it-matters.html) walks through the markup.

**8. Re-score after six weeks:** a YMYL audit is a loop, not a one-time project. The second pass always reveals something the first missed.

## Mistakes We Nearly Made

A few traps showed up while we worked, and they are common enough to name.

- **Treating YMYL as someone else's problem.** If your content touches money, safety, or a big decision, it is yours.
- **Adding bios you cannot back.** A fake credential is worse than no credential. Say what is true.
- **Writing a policy you do not follow.** A review promise you ignore is a trust leak, not a trust signal.
- **Hiding the AI disclosure.** If tools help draft your content, say so plainly and say a human checks it.
- **Chasing volume.** Publishing more posts does not fix a weak byline. It multiplies it.

None of these are exotic. They are the defaults when a team grows faster than its process, which is exactly why the audit is worth running before someone else runs it on you.

## Key Takeaways

- YMYL is about the potential for harm, not your industry label, so marketing and SEO sites are often in scope.
- A YMYL audit is an evidence hunt. You need a named author, verifiable credentials, cited sources, a review date, and a disclaimer on every risky page.
- Our own audit found the modified-date gap closed, the named-author share improving, and three hard gaps still open: no named reviewer, no visible review date, and no published address.
- You can run the same audit without paid tools, using a page list, a five-signal rubric, and an afternoon.
- Fix the cheap wins first (author box, review date, About page). Then handle ads, schema, and your review schedule.
- Treat it as a loop. Score, fix, review, and score again.

## Conclusion

The most useful thing a YMYL audit does is remove the guesswork. You stop hoping your site looks trustworthy and start counting the signals a rater, a reader, or an AI assistant can actually see. For a marketing site like ours, that turned a vague worry into a short list we can work through.

Start with your highest-risk pages. Name a human. Show why they are qualified. Date the review. Keep the ads honest and calm. None of it is glamorous, and all of it compounds.
