You open Search Console on a Monday morning, and the graph looks different. Clicks are down, or maybe up, or bouncing around like a nervous heartbeat. Your first instinct is to start changing things. Rewrite the homepage. Delete old blog posts. Email your developer at 7 a.m.

Please do not do that yet.

Google started rolling out its **September 2026 spam update** on September 24, and the rollout window is longer than usual. Rankings tend to move around while an update is still in progress, and some of those moves reverse. Panic edits during a rollout can make a clean site worse and still not fix a real problem.

In this guide, we explain what Google has actually confirmed, what it has not said, which spam policies matter most, how to check whether you were affected, and what to do first. We also separate the facts from the speculation, because there is a lot of both floating around right now.

## Google's September 2026 Spam Update: The Confirmed Facts

Here is what Google's own Search Status Dashboard and trustworthy industry coverage agree on.

| Question | Confirmed answer |
|---|---|
| **When did it start?** | September 24, 2026, at 9:15 a.m. Pacific time |
| **Where does it apply?** | Globally, in all languages |
| **How long will it take?** | Google says it may take up to two weeks (one analysis points to around October 8) |
| **Is it a core update?** | No. It is a spam update |
| **Did Google add new spam policies?** | No. Google described it as a normal spam update |
| **Did Google name a specific target?** | No. Google has not said which spam policy or tactic is the focus |
| **How many spam updates this year?** | It is the fourth in 2026, after March, June, and August |

The dashboard entry itself is only two sentences long. There is no blog post and no list of targeted tactics. As of the most recent reports we found, which date from September 28 to 30, Google still listed the rollout as in progress. **Check the Search Status Dashboard for the current status**, because it may have changed since we wrote this.

### How this update compares with the other 2026 spam updates

| Update | Started | Rollout time |
|---|---|---|
| March 2026 spam update | March 24 | About 19 hours 30 minutes |
| June 2026 spam update | June 24 | About 2 days 1 hour |
| August 2026 spam update | August 18 | About 2 days 16 hours |
| **September 2026 spam update** | **September 24** | **Up to 2 weeks (longest window of the year)** |

Reporters also noted that it arrived 37 days after the August update started, which is the shortest gap between spam updates on Google's dashboard. In plain terms, Google is tuning its spam defenses more often. ## Spam Update vs Core Update: What Is the Difference?

Business owners often mix these up, so here is the simple version.

| | Spam update | Core update |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Improves systems that detect and demote spam | Broad reassessment of how Google ranks helpful content |
| **Who feels it most** | Sites that break Google's spam policies | Any site, including honest ones |
| **Typical cause of a drop** | Something on your site resembles a policy violation | Competitors improved, or Google re-weighed signals |
| **Penalty type** | Algorithmic, applied automatically | Algorithmic, applied automatically |

A spam update is not a manual penalty. Nobody at Google reviewed your site and clicked a button. If your site follows Google's spam policies, this update should not have affected you in a harmful way, though normal ranking movement can still happen.

## What Has Google Not Said? (And Why That Matters)

Because Google has not named a specific target, you will see some articles confidently claiming the update "targets X." Be careful with those. Two examples:

- Some outlets report that the update does **not** target link spam. Google has not detailed this in its announcement, and the claim comes from industry reporting, so treat it as unconfirmed.
- Some SEOs link the update to Google's SAFE system for detecting low quality AI content. Coverage notes that Google has not said SAFE is the mechanism behind this update.

What we can say with confidence is this: **a spam update enforces Google's existing spam policies through its automated systems.** So the smartest way to prepare is to review those policies, not to guess at one hidden tactic.

## Google's Spam Policies in Plain English

These are the practices Google's published policies describe as spam. The ones highlighted by many SEO educators during this rollout are **doorway pages, scaled content abuse, expired domain abuse, and site reputation abuse**, so we start there.

| Policy | What it means | Local business example |
|---|---|---|
| **Doorway abuse** | Many near identical pages built to rank for slight keyword variations and funnel visitors to one place | 150 pages like "plumber in [every suburb]" with swapped place names |
| **Scaled content abuse** | Producing lots of pages mainly to manipulate rankings, with little value to users, however they are made | Hundreds of auto written articles on topics the business knows nothing about |
| **Expired domain abuse** | Buying an expired domain mainly to borrow its past reputation for low quality content | A new "health tips" site on a bought domain that used to be a university department |
| **Site reputation abuse** | Hosting low value third party content on a trusted site to ride its ranking power | A respected site hosting loads of unrelated coupon or casino pages with little oversight |
| **Cloaking** | Showing search engines different content than visitors see | Showing Google a text rich page and users a short sales page |
| **Hidden text and links** | Text or links placed where people cannot see them, to influence rankings | White text on white background stuffed with keywords |
| **Keyword stuffing** | Repeating keywords unnaturally | "Best dentist Pune best dentist Pune cheap" repeated in a paragraph |
| **Link spam** | Paid links, link exchanges, and low quality links meant to manipulate rankings | Buying 500 links from a vendor |
| **Thin affiliate pages** | Affiliate content that adds nothing beyond a manufacturer's product description | Copied product blurbs with affiliate links and no original review |
| **Sneaky redirects** | Sending users somewhere unexpected | A page that redirects mobile visitors to an unrelated ad site |
| **Scraping** | Copying content from other sites with no added value | Republishing competitor blog posts |
| **User generated spam** | Spammy comments or profiles left unmoderated | A forum full of casino links |
| **Hacked content** | Spam injected into your site by attackers | Pharmacy links hidden in old blog posts |

### A closer look at the four headline policies

**Doorway pages.** Google's long standing concern is a bulk set of pages that exist to catch searches rather than help people. For local businesses this usually shows up as "service plus city" pages. A page for a city where you genuinely work, with real local details such as projects, reviews, and staff, is fine. A hundred pages with only the city name changed is not.

**Scaled content abuse.** Google introduced this policy in 2024, and noted that whether content is produced by pure automation is not always clear in today's methods. The policy looks at the **purpose and value**, not the tool. We cover this in more detail below.

**Expired domain abuse.** Google says expired domains purchased and repurposed mainly to boost the ranking of low quality or unoriginal content count as spam. Buying an old domain and building something real and relevant is a different story. If you are choosing a domain for a genuine business, our [guide to buying a domain name for your business](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/29/buying-domain-name-for-business-guide.html) is a good starting point.

**Site reputation abuse.** Google treats very low value third party content produced mainly for ranking purposes, without close oversight by the site owner, as spam. This is the idea behind what many call parasite SEO. We explain it, and whether a business should ever use it, in our post on [what parasite SEO is](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/17/what-is-parasite-seo-should-business-use-it.html).

## What About AI Generated Content?

This is the question we get most, so here is the clear answer.

**Using AI is not against Google's spam policies.** What the policies target is **mass producing low value content to manipulate rankings**. A business owner who uses AI to help outline an article, then adds real experience, checks the facts, and edits in their own voice is doing something very different from someone who generates 2,000 thin pages overnight.

A simple test: *If AI did not exist, would this page still deserve to exist?* If it exists only because it was cheap to produce at scale, that is a warning sign.

### Safe versus risky AI use

| Safer use | Riskier use |
|---|---|
| Brainstorming topics and outlines | Publishing raw output without review |
| Editing and tightening your own writing | Generating hundreds of near duplicate pages |
| Summarising your own research and data | Writing about topics where you have no expertise |
| Drafting FAQs from real customer questions | Auto generating location pages by changing the city name |
| Translating and checking your content | Spinning other people's articles |

## Step 1: Do Not Panic During the Rollout

This is the most important instruction, and the one people ignore.

- **Rankings can swing both ways mid rollout.** Some changes reverse before the update finishes.
- **Early data is incomplete.** Judging damage on day three of a two week rollout is like judging a cricket match after the first over.
- **Panic edits create new variables.** If you change twenty things, you will never know which one mattered.

**What to do instead:** keep a simple log. Write down the date you noticed a change, which pages or queries moved, and any site changes you made recently. Then wait for Google to mark the rollout as complete on the Search Status Dashboard.

## Step 2: Find Out Whether You Were Actually Affected

A traffic drop does not automatically mean the spam update hit you. Work through this checklist.

### 2.1 Check the dates

Open Search Console, go to **Performance**, and compare the period **before September 24** with the period **during the rollout**. If your traffic fell before September 24, the spam update is probably not the cause. A fair comparison needs full weeks, not individual days.

### 2.2 Rule out technical problems

Before blaming Google, check the boring stuff:

- Did you change themes, plugins, or hosting recently?
- Are important pages returning errors, or accidentally set to "noindex"?
- Did a robots.txt change block part of your site?
- Is your analytics tracking still working? (A broken tag looks exactly like a traffic drop.)
- Did seasonality or a festival period shift demand?

Our [monthly website audit checklist](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/03/monthly-website-audit-checklist.html) covers each of these, and our write up of [real findings from a monthly website audit](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/10/01/monthly-website-audit-real-findings.html) shows how often small technical issues are the real culprit.

### 2.3 Check for a manual action

In Search Console, open the **Manual actions** report. A spam update is algorithmic, so you should normally see no manual action. If you do see one, that is a separate issue with its own reconsideration process.

### 2.4 Segment the damage

Look at which pages, queries, and countries lost traffic.

| What you see | What it may suggest |
|---|---|
| Drops concentrated on a group of mass produced pages | Possible doorway or scaled content issue |
| Drops only on affiliate or comparison pages | Possible thin affiliate content issue |
| Drops on one section hosting outside contributors | Possible site reputation concern |
| Sitewide drop across all page types | Could be technical, seasonal, or broader quality signals |
| One or two pages down, rest stable | Likely normal ranking movement |

### 2.5 Check search intent

Search your main keywords yourself. Does your page really answer what the person wants? Pages that miss intent can slide even without any spam. Our guide to [decoding search intent](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/21/decoding-search-intent-what-customers-want.html) helps you check this properly.

## Step 3: Audit Your Site Against the Spam Policies

Once the rollout settles, run a calm, structured audit. Here is a practical framework.

| Area | Red flags to look for | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Location and service pages** | Dozens of pages that differ only by city or keyword | Merge, rewrite with real local detail, or remove and redirect |
| **Blog content** | Thin, repetitive, or unreviewed AI drafts | Improve with original insight, or consolidate and redirect |
| **Link profile** | Paid links, link swaps, sudden bursts of low quality links | Stop buying, remove what you can, document the rest |
| **Hidden text and links** | Text matching background colour, tiny fonts, off screen elements | Remove it |
| **Cloaking and redirects** | Different content for Googlebot, odd mobile redirects | Fix so users and Google see the same thing |
| **Affiliate pages** | Copied product descriptions, no original review or testing | Add genuine experience, photos, and comparisons, or cut them |
| **Third party content** | Guest posts or sections you do not review closely | Add editorial oversight, or remove |
| **Comments and forums** | Spam links left unmoderated | Moderate and block spam |
| **Domain history** | A bought domain with unrelated past content | Review what the site now offers |
| **Security** | Unknown pages, injected links, strange redirects | Scan, clean up, and update everything |

## Step 4: Fix Content the Right Way

If your audit finds weak spots, here is how to handle them.

1. **Improve before you delete.** If a page has a real purpose, rewrite it with original information.
2. **Consolidate near duplicates.** Merge several thin pages into one strong page, and redirect the old URLs.
3. **Remove what has no reason to exist.** Pages made only to catch searches should go.
4. **Add real expertise.** Include author names, credentials, first hand photos, case studies, and customer stories.
5. **Cite sources and keep facts current.**
6. **Revive worthwhile old content** using a systematic process, as we explain in our guide to [reviving dead content with audits](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/20/reviving-dead-content-systematic-audits.html).

### Build trust signals that last

Spam updates reward honest, helpful sites over time. Strengthen the basics:

- Clear About and Contact pages
- Real business address and phone number
- Genuine reviews
- Transparent policies

## Step 5: Be Patient With Recovery

If a site was demoted for breaking spam policies, fixing the problem does not bring rankings back overnight. Google's automated systems need time to re-evaluate a site after you improve it. Expect a gradual process, not a switch flipping.

Keep monitoring, keep your log updated, and avoid undoing good changes just because results are slow. Also, measure the right things. Rankings alone can mislead, as we explain in our post on [SEO metrics beyond rankings](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/19/seo-metrics-beyond-rankings.html).
