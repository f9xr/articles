# United Airlines Lets Search Engines In, But Makes AI Agents Ask First

*By F9XR Team | Published November 15, 2026 | 7 min read*

Picture a very polite bouncer. Google walks up to the door and gets waved straight in. A person with a regular browser walks in too. Then an AI assistant shows up, ready to book a ticket on someone's behalf, and the bouncer says, "Do you have written permission?"

## What United Actually Changed

Here is the short version of what the airline did and when.

**The announcement.** United said on November 11, 2026 that it is restricting unauthorized third-party AI agents on its own platforms and through travel agencies and booking sites. It updated its website terms and its contract of carriage on the same day. Search Engine Journal's Matt Southern broke down the fine print on November 14.

## Who Is Allowed In and Who Is Not

This table sums up how United's terms treat different visitors, based on reporting from Search Engine Journal and United's published FAQ.

| Visitor or activity | What United's terms say |
|---|---|
| Search engines indexing public pages | Allowed (exempt) |
| A person using a standard web browser | Allowed |
| Assistive technology | Allowed |
| AI agent, bot, scraper or browser extension that searches, books, buys or manages on the site or app | Needs written permission |
| A browser's built-in AI agent feature | Not exempt, needs permission |
| Using United site data to train, fine-tune, ground or evaluate an AI model | Barred |
| Answers an AI system builds from a search engine's index | Not addressed in the terms |

## Why United Drew This Line

United gave a few reasons, and they are worth reading closely because small businesses can borrow the thinking even if they cannot copy the policy.

* **Account security.** United says handing login details to an outside AI assistant can expose sensitive customer information.
* **Unintended actions.** The airline pointed to the risk of accidental purchases, account changes or misuse of MileagePlus miles.

## Why robots.txt Is Not Enough Anymore

For years, robots.txt was the main way to tell crawlers what to do. It is still useful, but it has a built-in weakness: it only works on bots that announce themselves and choose to follow the rules.


## What This Means for Business Owners, Startups and Local Businesses

Here is where it gets practical. United's situation is not your situation, and copying its rules blindly could hurt you.

### If you run a local business

You probably want AI tools to find you, understand you and recommend you. When someone asks ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude or Perplexity for the best dentist, plumber or restaurant near them, you want to be in that answer. Blocking everything AI related would be like taking your sign off the street.


## The Three Layers of Control

Think of your website rules as three layers. United used two of them heavily. Most small sites only use one.

| Layer | What it does | Where it falls short |
|---|---|---|
| robots.txt | Asks well-behaved crawlers to skip certain pages | Only works on bots that identify themselves and cooperate |
| Terms of service | Sets legal rules for visitors, agents and the people behind them | Does not physically stop anyone, and enforcement takes effort |
| Bot protection and rate limits | Technically blocks or slows suspicious automated traffic | Can accidentally block real customers or good crawlers if set too tight |

The strongest setups use all three and make sure they say the same thing. A robots.txt that welcomes everyone, terms that ban everyone and a firewall that blocks half your real visitors is a mess.

## A Practical Playbook You Can Use This Week

You do not need a legal team to start. Work through these steps in order.

1. **Write your stance in one sentence.** For example: "We want search engines and AI assistants to read our public pages, but only our own checkout can process orders." Everything else follows from that sentence.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

* **Blocking all bots out of fear.** You might vanish from search results and AI answers in one move.
* **Assuming robots.txt covers AI agents.** As United's case shows, many agents never read it.
* **Copying a big brand's terms word for word.** Their risks and your risks are not the same.
* **Letting robots.txt, terms and firewall contradict each other.** Mixed signals confuse crawlers and cause real customers trouble.
* **Setting it and forgetting it.** The rules around AI agents are changing month to month.

## Frequently Asked Questions

#### Did United Airlines ban AI agents?

United did not ban all AI use. It requires written permission for AI agents, bots and similar tools that search, book, buy or manage anything on united.com or its app. Using AI to research and plan a trip is still fine as long as the tool does not access United's site or app. United says it does not work with any third-party AI agents yet.

#### Can search engines still index United's website?

Yes. United's website terms exempt search engines that index its public pages. The terms do not say how they treat answers that AI systems create from search engine indexes.

#### Why doesn't United use robots.txt to block AI agents?

Many personal AI agents do not publish a user agent that a robots.txt rule could name, and some act like a normal browser visit. As of November 14, 2026, United's robots.txt file named no AI crawlers or agents, so the airline relies on its legal terms and contract instead.


## Conclusion

United's move is less about airlines and more about a question every website will face: do you want to be read, or do you want to be operated? Search engines and customers browsing normally are welcome. Software that logs in, books and buys on someone's behalf has to ask first.

For most small and local businesses, the smart takeaway is balance. Stay visible to search and AI answers, protect the parts of your site where money and personal data live, and keep your rules consistent across robots.txt, terms and security settings.
