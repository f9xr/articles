A single line in United Airlines' website terms now does something a firewall cannot. It separates two things the web has treated as one for almost 30 years: being read, and being operated.

On November 11, 2026, United updated its website terms and contract of carriage. Search engines can still index its public pages. Any AI agent that searches, books, buys or manages a trip on united.com or its app needs written permission first. United Airlines AI agents now need a signature before they can act on a booking.

For a local business owner, that might sound like airline paperwork. In practice, United has written down a question every website will face: who gets to look at your public pages, and who gets to act on them.

> **Quick answer:** United Airlines now allows search engines to index its public pages but requires written permission for AI agents that search, book or manage anything on united.com or its app. The terms also bar using United's data to train or ground AI models. United's robots.txt file named no AI crawlers as of November 14, so the rules live in legal terms, not in robots.txt.

## What United Airlines Actually Changed

The announcement is short and specific. United said it is restricting unauthorized third-party AI agents on its own platforms and through travel agencies and booking sites. It also updated its [website terms](https://www.united.com/en/us/fly/legal.html) and its contract of carriage on the same day.

Here are the four moving parts, based on [Search Engine Journal's report](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/united-ai-agents-permission-search-indexing/597418/) and [United's own announcement](https://united.mediaroom.com/news-releases?item=125496).

**The definition is broad.** An "Automated Agent" covers AI assistants, bots, scrapers, browser extensions and AI features built into browsers or apps. It applies even when a person supervises or approves each action. No such agent may access the site or app without prior written permission, and United can block or slow one down at any time.

**The contract has teeth.** Rule 6.J.6 adds using AI agents to make, change or service a ticket to the list of prohibited practices, next to hidden-city ticketing. Rule 5.H says United can cancel bookings made or managed by AI agents without notice.

**Travel agencies are included.** According to [United's Digital Trust Center FAQ](https://www.united.com/en/us/fly/help-center/digital-trust-center/artificial-intelligence/third-party-ai-agents.html), agencies and booking platforms cannot use third-party AI agents to search, book or manage United travel for customers. Agencies also need approval before using their own AI tools.

**Old bookings survive.** United says it does not work with any third-party AI agents yet, and bookings that agents made before November 11 will not be canceled. That is a sensible grace period, and it tells you the policy is aimed at future access rather than punishing past customers.

Notice what is missing. There is no technical specification, no user agent list, and no detail on how United will detect an agent. This is a legal boundary, not a server rule.

## Who Gets In and Who Needs Permission

United's terms sort visitors into groups. The table below is the practical version.

| Visitor or activity | What United's terms say |
|---|---|
| Search engines indexing public pages | Allowed (exempt) |
| A person using a standard web browser | Allowed |
| Assistive technology | Allowed |
| AI agent, bot, scraper or browser extension that searches, books, buys or manages on the site or app | Needs written permission |
| A browser's built-in AI agent feature | Not exempt, needs permission |
| Using United data to train, fine-tune, ground or evaluate an AI model | Barred |
| Answers an AI system builds from a search engine's index | Not addressed |
| Travel agencies using third-party AI agents | Not permitted without United's approval |
| Using AI to research and plan a trip without touching united.com or the app | Allowed |

The quiet row is the seventh one. United's terms say nothing about what happens when an AI assistant answers a travel question using information it pulled from a search index rather than from United directly. That gray area will be tested, probably by an agent maker rather than an airline.

The distinction is simple. Indexing is reading. Booking is acting. United kept the first open and closed the second.

## Why United Airlines AI Agents Need Permission

United gave four reasons, and each one is financial before it is philosophical.

**Account security.** Sharing a login with an outside AI assistant can expose sensitive customer information. A compromised MileagePlus account has a real balance attached to it.

**Unintended actions.** United pointed to accidental purchases, account changes and misuse of loyalty miles. Every one of those creates a refund, a chargeback or a support ticket.

**Regulation.** Automated actions can clash with FAA and Department of Transportation rules. United's example is check-in, where a person must confirm that their bags contain no banned items. Software cannot make that legal acknowledgment for you.

**Control of the customer relationship.** CEO Scott Kirby told the Wall Street Journal, "I don't want to create another middleman."

That last reason is the commercial one. An agent that books on a customer's behalf sits between the airline and the passenger. It can compare fares, steer the choice and collect the relationship. United would rather keep that surface for itself.

The pattern matters. None of these reasons is about hiding public information. They are about protecting transactions, accounts and margin. That distinction is the whole lesson for a smaller business.

## The Cost Side of Agent Access

Think about what a wrong automated action actually costs you. A booking change can trigger a refund, a payment processor dispute, a support conversation and a correction to a customer record. A price scraper can force you to rewrite your pricing logic. A login attempt on a member account can lock out the real owner on a Friday evening.

None of those costs appear in robots.txt. That file only asks a well-behaved crawler to skip a path. It does not verify who is acting, whether a human approved the action, or whether the action is allowed by your terms.

The return side is different. AI assistants now answer buying questions, and a business that cannot be read by those systems loses a discovery channel. For a local shop, the upside of being quoted accurately in an AI answer is larger than the risk of a bot reading the opening hours.

So the decision is not "block AI" or "allow AI." It is a split: open the pages that create demand, protect the places where money and identity move. United did exactly that, at airline scale.

## Why robots.txt Is Not Enough Anymore

For years, robots.txt was the main way to tell crawlers what to do. It still works for search engines. Google's own [robots.txt introduction](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/robots/intro) explains the limits clearly: the file is a request, not an enforcement mechanism.

Personal AI agents make that weakness visible. Many run inside a browser, some look like a normal human visit, and some publish no user agent that a rule could name. As of November 14, United's robots.txt named no AI crawlers or agents at all. The airline put its rules in its terms and contract instead.

United is not alone. Amazon blocked Meta's Muse shopping agent in September based on its Conditions of Use, which bind the shopper rather than Meta. We covered that in our post on [Amazon blocking Meta's Muse AI assistant](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/29/amazon-blocks-meta-muse-ai-assistant.html). eBay added "buy-for-me agents" and LLM-driven bots to the automated tools that need its permission in an update posted in January.

The direction is clear. Large platforms are moving from "please do not crawl this path" to "here is what you are legally allowed to do here." A small business does not need a legal department to follow the same logic, but it does need to decide where the line sits.

## What This Means for Your Website

United's risk profile is not yours. Copying its rules word for word could hurt a business that depends on being found. Here is the practical split.

### If you run a local business

You want AI tools to find you, understand you and recommend you. When someone asks ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude or Perplexity for the best dentist, plumber or restaurant nearby, you want to be in that answer. Blocking every AI visitor would be like taking your sign off the street.

Keep your homepage, services, location, hours, prices and FAQs open and easy to read. Our guide on [how AI picks local businesses](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/16/how-ai-picks-local-businesses-2026.html) shows what those systems actually look at. Protect the accounts and booking forms, not the menu.

### If you run a startup or an online store

Your situation sits in the middle. You want product pages indexed and visible, and you may also have pricing logic, member areas or proprietary data you would rather not hand to every scraper. AI shopping agents are coming, and some will try to buy on a customer's behalf.

Decide now whether that is welcome, and under what conditions. The policy question is separate: do agents get to complete a checkout, or only to read the catalog?

### If you publish content

If your business depends on original articles, reports or data, the "no training or grounding" language in United's terms is worth noticing. It is a legal statement of intent. Whether it holds up in practice is unresolved.

## The Three Layers of Control

Most small sites use one layer, usually robots.txt. The stronger setups use all three and make sure they agree.

| Layer | What it does | Where it fails | What it costs to run |
|---|---|---|---|
| robots.txt | Asks well-behaved crawlers to skip certain pages | Only works on bots that identify themselves and cooperate | About 10 minutes to review |
| Terms of service | Sets legal rules for visitors, agents and the people behind them | Does not physically stop anyone, and enforcement takes effort | A lawyer's review, plus a yearly check |
| Bot protection and rate limits | Technically blocks or slows suspicious automated traffic | Can block real customers or good crawlers if set too tight | A monthly fee, plus tuning time |

The contradiction test is simple. Read your robots.txt, your terms and your firewall settings side by side. If robots.txt welcomes everyone, the terms ban everyone and the firewall blocks half your real visitors, you have three policies and no strategy.

United chose legal terms because its problem is transactional. A local bakery might choose open pages and tight checkout protection. Both are valid. Mixed signals are not.

## A Practical Playbook for This Week

You do not need a legal team to start. Work through these steps in order.

1. **Write your stance in one sentence.** For example: "We want search engines and AI assistants to read our public pages, but only our own checkout can process orders." Every other decision follows from that sentence.
2. **Open your robots.txt.** Visit yourdomain.com/robots.txt. Confirm you are not blocking Googlebot or Bingbot by accident. Then decide deliberately about AI crawlers.
3. **Read your own terms of service.** Many small business sites run a template nobody has opened since launch. If you take bookings, payments or logins, ask a lawyer whether a short clause on automated agents makes sense.
4. **Protect the places where money and data move.** Logins, booking forms, checkout and account pages deserve rate limits, bot protection and strong authentication. Public pages should stay open.
5. **Make public pages easy to read.** Use clear headings, consistent business details, FAQ content and structured data. This helps search engines and AI answers get the facts right.
6. **Watch your traffic.** Check server logs or analytics for odd user agents, repeated hits on pricing pages and traffic spikes at unusual hours.
7. **Review it every quarter.** United says it will keep reviewing how AI assistants work with its services. Your policy should not be set in stone either.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Blocking every bot out of fear.** You can vanish from search results and AI answers in one move.
- **Assuming robots.txt covers AI agents.** United's case shows that many agents never read it.
- **Copying a large brand's terms word for word.** Its risks, regulators and margins are not yours.
- **Letting robots.txt, terms and firewall contradict each other.** Mixed signals confuse crawlers and frustrate real customers.
- **Setting it and forgetting it.** The rules around AI agents are changing month to month.

## What to Watch Next

Two questions are still open. First, United's materials do not explain how it detects an agent, so enforcement remains unclear. Second, it is not yet known whether other airlines or large retailers will write similar rules. If they do, expect more sites to separate indexing from agent access.

A note on numbers: this story is days old, and there is no reliable dataset on how many sites have adopted similar policies. Be skeptical of any "X percent of websites" claim you see this week. The solid examples so far are United, Amazon and eBay.
