If you manage Google Ads for your business, even casually, you are going to want to read this one carefully. Google just confirmed a change that quietly removes a setting many advertisers have relied on for years without thinking twice about it: campaign-level language targeting in Search campaigns.

Starting in late September 2026, that manual control disappears. No more picking "English" or "Spanish" from a dropdown menu and calling it done. Google's own automated systems will now decide which language a searcher understands and match your ad accordingly, based on the language of your ad copy and signals about the user.

For a lot of business owners, this sounds like a small, technical footnote. It is not. If you run a bilingual business, operate in a multilingual city, sell to international customers, or simply built your account structure around language-based campaigns years ago, this change touches your budget, your targeting accuracy, and potentially your cost per lead.

Here is what is changing, why Google is doing it, whether it helps or hurts your campaigns, and what to do about it before the September deadline. No jargon, no fluff, just what you need to know and do.

---

## What Is Actually Changing in Google Ads Language Targeting

Here are the facts, since there has been some confusion floating around. Google actually announced something similar back in August 2025 with a deadline that quietly slipped.

Google [confirmed this update](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/1722078) again on August 13, 2026, with the change rolling out in late September 2026. Here is the breakdown.

### For Search Campaigns

The campaign-level language targeting setting is being removed entirely. Search ads will automatically match based on the language of your ad, combined with Google's own signals about what language a user understands.

### For Performance Max Campaigns

This one is a bit more layered. The campaign-level language setting will no longer apply to ads shown on the Google Search Network within Performance Max. Search Network placements inside Performance Max will behave just like regular Search campaigns now. However, your language settings will still apply to other Performance Max channels, including YouTube, Display, Discover, and Gmail. Shopping ads within Performance Max are not affected by language settings at all.

### For Existing Campaigns

If you already run single-language campaign structures, say a dedicated English campaign and a separate Spanish one, those will keep functioning normally. You can optionally strip out the old language criteria for tidiness, but Google says it is not required since the setting simply will not apply going forward.

### For API Users

If you or your agency manages campaigns programmatically through the Google Ads API, you should stop setting language criteria on new Search campaigns, since that setting is being phased out on the backend too.

### Quick Reference Table

| Campaign Type | What Happens |
|---|---|
| Standard Search campaigns | Language targeting setting removed. Matching runs on ad language plus user signals |
| AI Max for Search | Same as above, campaign-level language setting removed |
| Performance Max (Search Network placements) | Language setting no longer applies here, behaves like standard Search |
| Performance Max (YouTube, Display, Discover, Gmail) | Language setting still applies, unaffected by this change |
| Performance Max (Shopping ads) | Not affected by language settings |
| Existing single-language campaigns | Continue working as before, no restructuring required |

## Why Is Google Doing This

Google's official reasoning, as outlined in their [language targeting documentation](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2756257?hl=en) and [ developer blog announcement](https://ads-developers.googleblog.com/2026/08/google-ads-language-targeting-changes.html), centers on accuracy and automation. Instead of relying on advertisers to manually flag which languages to target, an approach that has always been a bit blunt, Google wants its AI to read the actual language of your ad and landing page, then match it to real user context and preferences at the moment of the search.

There is a reasonable argument here. Manual language targeting has always had quirks, much like the challenge of optimizing for platforms beyond Google alone, which our [Search Everywhere optimization guide](https://f9xr.github.io/articles/2026/08/14/search-everywhere-optimization-youtube-google-seo.html) covers in detail. Someone searching in English while living in a Spanish-speaking household, a bilingual user switching languages mid-session, or a business running ads in a region with three official languages. These situations were never handled perfectly by a single dropdown setting.

This move also fits a much bigger pattern. Over the past couple of years, Google Ads has steadily been shifting manual controls into AI-driven, automated systems, from the rise of Performance Max itself to broad match defaults, automatically created assets, and AI-generated ad copy. Removing manual language targeting is really just the latest step in that same direction, one that parallels the broader shift toward [zero-click search](https://f9xr.github.io/articles/2026/08/16/zero-click-search-google-business-profile-homepage.html) where Google increasingly resolves user intent without sending traffic elsewhere.

That said, this exact change was first announced back in August 2025 with a deadline of "end of 2025." That deadline came and went without action. Google is now back with a new date and a wider scope that includes AI Max for Search. Treat announced timelines with skepticism, but plan as though this one is real.

## Is This Good or Bad for Advertisers

This is the part everyone actually wants to know, and the honest answer is: it depends on your business, but there are legitimate concerns on both sides.

### The Case For This Being a Good Change

* **Better matching in multilingual contexts.** If your customers switch between languages, or if your existing dropdown setting was already an imperfect proxy for who is actually searching, automated matching based on real signals could mean fewer missed impressions.
* **Less manual maintenance.** One less setting to configure, audit, and troubleshoot when campaigns underperform.
* **Consistency with how Google already partially works.** Google has used both campaign settings and its own language signals together for a while now. This just removes the manual half of that equation.

### The Case For This Being a Concerning Change

* **Loss of control.** This is the big one. Advertisers who deliberately used language targeting to segment budgets, run different offers per language group, or exclude certain languages entirely are losing a lever they relied on.
* **Language exclusions still are not supported.** If you were hoping this update would let you block ads from serving to certain language audiences, that capability is not part of the change. It simply is not supported, before or after.
* **Multilingual businesses lose granular budget control.** If you ran separate campaigns per language specifically to control spend and performance by language segment, that structure now depends entirely on ad copy language and Google's signal accuracy, which is not something you can audit or adjust directly.
* **A pattern of shrinking manual controls.** Individually, this change is incremental. But stacked on top of previous changes like automatically created assets, campaign-level broad match defaults, and Performance Max's already limited manual levers, it adds up. [Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/) and [PPC Land](https://ppcland.com/) have both noted this is part of a broader, ongoing trend of Google narrowing advertiser control in favor of automated signals.

So, good or bad? For simple, single-language businesses targeting one clear market, this is likely a non-event. For multilingual businesses, agencies managing complex international accounts, or advertisers who used language settings for reasons beyond pure language targeting, this is a real loss of control worth planning around.

## What Business Owners Should Do Now

You do not need to panic, but you do need a plan. Here is a practical checklist to work through before late September 2026.

### 1. Audit Your Current Language Targeting Setup

Go through every active Search campaign and Performance Max campaign and note where language targeting is currently being used, and why. Was it for language matching, or was it being used as an indirect budget or audience segmentation tool?

### 2. Align Ad Copy and Landing Pages With Intended Language

Since matching will now lean heavily on the actual language of your ads and landing pages, make sure every ad group's creative is clearly and consistently written in the language you want to target. Mixed-language ad copy could confuse the automated matching signal.

### 3. Keep Single-Language Campaign Structures If They Are Working

If you already run separate campaigns per language, you do not need to restructure anything right away. These will continue functioning normally. Just do not expect the old dropdown setting to do anything meaningful going forward.

### 4. Watch Performance Closely After the Rollout

Set a calendar reminder for early October 2026 to review impression share, click through rate, and conversion data by campaign. Compare it against September's baseline so you can spot any real shifts in language matching accuracy early.

### 5. Talk to Your Agency or PPC Manager Now, Not Later

If someone else manages your account, ask them directly how they are preparing for this change and whether your account structure needs any adjustments before the rollout.

### 6. Do Not Rely on Language Settings for Exclusion Purposes

If you were using language targeting to prevent ads from showing to certain audiences, that was never a fully reliable exclusion method anyway, and it is becoming even less relevant now. Look into negative keywords, audience exclusions, and geographic targeting refinements instead.

## Alternatives and Workarounds Worth Considering

Since manual language targeting is going away, here is what business owners can lean on instead to maintain control over who sees their ads.

### Geographic and Location Targeting

Layering precise location targeting on top of language-aligned ad copy can help narrow your audience meaningfully, especially for local businesses serving specific neighborhoods or regions with known language patterns.

### Audience Signals and Custom Segments

Google Ads allows you to layer audience signals, in-market segments, and custom intent audiences on top of your Search and Performance Max campaigns. These will not replace language targeting directly, but they add another layer of relevance control.

### Ad Copy Localization

Since ad language is now the primary matching signal, doubling down on properly localized, professionally translated ad copy (not just direct machine translation) becomes more important than ever. This is not just an SEO best practice, it is now a direct targeting mechanism.

### Landing Page Language Consistency

Make sure your landing pages match your ad language exactly. Mismatched language between ad and landing page was always a quality score risk, but now it may also affect how accurately Google matches your ad to the right audience in the first place.

### Manual Campaign Segmentation by Region Instead of Language

For businesses previously segmenting budgets by language, consider whether segmenting by geographic region achieves a similar practical outcome, since location and language often correlate closely in local and regional markets.

## What This Means Specifically for Local and Small Businesses

If you are running a local service business, a neighborhood shop, or a small multilingual team, here is the practical takeaway.

Most small, single-language local businesses will not notice much difference at all. If you have always run one campaign in one language for one local market, this change mostly happens behind the scenes.

Where it matters more is for businesses in multilingual areas, cities with large bilingual populations, border regions, or tourist-heavy areas where customers search in more than one language. If that is you, the smartest move is investing extra care into ad copy and landing page language consistency now, rather than waiting until after the rollout to react to a performance dip.

It is also a good moment to double check your Google Business Profile, website content, and landing pages for language consistency and quality overall. Local SEO and local Google Ads performance tend to move together. A fragmented multilingual web presence tends to hurt both.
