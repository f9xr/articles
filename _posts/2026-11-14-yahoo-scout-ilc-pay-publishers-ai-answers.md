Your best guide to wedding venue costs gets quoted in an AI answer. The reader never clicks, never sees your ad, and never books a tour. For two years that arrangement has been free for the AI company and expensive for the site that did the work.

The Yahoo Scout ILC agreement changes part of that. On November 12, 2026, the Internet Licensing Collective opened with Yahoo Scout as its first commercial licensee, so an AI answers tool has agreed to pay publishers when their content informs what it shows. The amounts are undisclosed, but the mechanism is public. Those two facts should decide how much attention you give this story.

If you run a small site with original content, the useful questions are narrower than the headlines suggest. Can you join? What rights would you be handing over? What should you fix before any money moves?

Here are the answers, followed by a week of work that pays off whether or not a check ever arrives.

---

## What the Yahoo Scout Deal Means for Publishers

The Internet Licensing Collective is a nonprofit rights organization built by publishers. It launched under that name on November 12, replacing the RSL Collective, the group behind the Really Simple Licensing standard. More than 3,000 media brands, content owners and platforms were part of the launch, according to the [ILC press release](https://internetcollective.org/press/ilc-launch-2026){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}. ILC's homepage now lists 3,100 publishers and 3.1 billion licensable works.

The structure borrows from music. Instead of every publisher negotiating separately with every AI company, the collective sells one blanket license and splits the money through published allocation rules. AI companies can license specific uses: training, retrieval, grounding, agents, AI overviews. Publishers opt in to the licenses they want.

Yahoo Scout gets ongoing access to permissioned content from participating publishers. Eric Feng, senior vice president and general manager of the Yahoo Research Group, put the reason plainly in the release: "AI answers are only as good as the sources behind them."

That framing is accurate, and it is also why the next section matters more than the announcement itself.

## How the Money Is Supposed to Work

Publishers want a number. Nobody has given one.

ILC co-founder Doug Leeds declined to disclose the terms of Yahoo's agreement to Digiday, as [Tomorrow's Publisher reported](https://tomorrowspublisher.today/news/yahoo-becomes-first-licensee-of-collective-ai-content-scheme-d02bdc1b9f81/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}. What the collective has published is the logic behind the split. ILC allocates revenue using three inputs: how a work contributes to an AI product, how often the AI relies on it, and the value of that AI product. A specialist source that grounds an answer should earn more than a large catalog that gets skimmed.

The same report describes a detail that matters for small publishers. ILC creates a separate royalty fund for each AI company rather than pooling all income. A medical site is paid from the fund tied to products that actually used its material, so a general search product cannot dilute its share.

The scale claim comes from Mather Economics, an independent analytics firm that was not paid by ILC. Its report, [The Price of Intelligence](https://www.mathereconomics.com/price-of-intelligence/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}, estimates AI companies would pay 15% to 35% of revenue to keep access to licensed content. That puts the market in the tens of billions, and Mather says it could pass US display advertising within three years.

Read the method before you read the headline. The estimate models willingness to pay using the run rates of OpenAI and Anthropic, not the whole AI industry, and it assumes demand keeps growing. It is a forecast, not a payout schedule.

One more fact complicates the good news. Yahoo CEO Jim Lanzone told HumanX in Amsterdam in September that micropayments from AI companies "will never be enough, even for someone like Reddit," and argued that search advertising should fund the open web instead. Yahoo also joined Microsoft's Publisher Content Marketplace pilot in January. Licensing through ILC is one revenue channel for Yahoo's partners, not a replacement for the traffic and ad model that came before it.

> **What we still do not know:** the fee, the per-publisher split, how small a publisher can be and still receive a meaningful payment, and which AI company signs next. ILC says this is just the beginning, and has not named anyone else.

A sensible rule for now: treat ILC income as zero in your budget, and treat the announcement as a signal about where licensing is going.

## Can a Small Site Actually Join?

Yes, according to ILC's own documentation. The [publisher FAQ](https://internetcollective.org/faq){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} says any website owner, publisher or creator who holds the rights to their online content can join, "from individual creators to large media companies." Membership is free.

The mechanics are lighter than most licensing deals. You create an account, publish an RSL declaration that states which sections of your site are available under which license, and choose the AI companies and uses you allow. You can change those settings at any time.

The declaration uses RSL, a machine-readable standard whose terms can cover AI training and search, including pay-per-crawl and pay-per-inference arrangements. ILC says there are no custom uploads or changes to how you publish, and that participation is non-exclusive, so existing direct deals stay in place and you are not included in a license you did not opt into.

What joining does not do is guarantee money. Membership makes your content eligible, and payment depends on whether licensed AI products actually use your work and how the allocation rules count that use. The launch list is dominated by large operations: The Guardian, Wikipedia, Stack Exchange, Ziff Davis titles, and newspaper groups. A five-page site for a dental clinic will not move a royalty pool on its own.

Join for the reporting and the optionality. The ILC platform is meant to show you how licensed products use your content, which is information you cannot get from server logs alone. Skip it if you are not prepared to read the license terms, because the terms decide what you are giving up.

## Yahoo Scout, AI Answers, and the Bing Connection

Yahoo Scout launched in beta on January 27, 2026, on desktop and inside the Yahoo Search app on iOS and Android, with a standalone site and app, according to [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/01/27/yahoo-scout-ai-search-launch){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}. The product shows answers with tables, images and inline citations. Its primary model is Anthropic's Claude, and it pulls open web sources through Microsoft Bing's grounding API.

The Bing detail is the practical one for a small site. If Scout assembles answers from Bing's index, your Bing presence affects whether a product like Scout can find and cite you. Yahoo says it has 250 million monthly users in the US, so this is not a niche surface.

Yahoo has also positioned Scout as publisher-friendly. Lanzone told [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/yahoo-scout-jim-lanzone-publishers-links-humanx){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} that Scout keeps many blue links to sources, partly because AI systems get things wrong and users need to check. Product details change, and a licensing deal does not oblige Scout to cite you. The index still decides that.

## What to Do This Week

### 1. Decide your AI access policy on purpose

Check your robots.txt and any bot-blocking rules in your CDN or security plugin. Many site owners block AI crawlers by accident after installing a security tool, then wonder why they never appear in AI answers. Decide which bots you want, then write it down and test the result. If you do want limits, our guide to [blocking AI crawlers on your site](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/04/block-ai-crawlers-on-your-site.html) explains the trade-offs.

### 2. Fix your ten best pages first

Pull the ten pages that already earn links, inquiries or rankings. For each, check four things:

- Does it contain something only you can say, such as your process, your data, your photos or a named expert?
- Is every fact current, and can a reader verify it from a source?
- Does it answer one clear question in the first two paragraphs?
- Would a stranger trust this page enough to call you?

Rewrite or merge the pages that fail. Deleting thin pages helps more than adding new ones.

### 3. Format for extraction, not just reading

AI systems quote passages, so make passages quotable. Use one clear H2 per question with a direct answer under it, and add a table where you compare options. Add FAQ entries through your CMS or front matter so the page emits valid structured data, and keep your business name, address and services identical on every page and profile. Our walkthrough on [making your business site visible to AI](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/14/steps-make-business-site-visible-to-ai.html) covers the setup.

### 4. Get the Bing basics right

Verify the site in Bing Webmaster Tools, submit your sitemap, and then check that key pages are indexed. When you publish something new, ping IndexNow. It notifies Bing, Yandex, Naver, Seznam and Yep, and it does nothing for Google, which does not participate. The whole job takes about half an hour.

### 5. Measure citations, not only rankings

Pick ten questions your customers ask. Once a month, run them through ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity and Scout. Record whether your brand appears, which page gets cited, and tag AI referrals in analytics so you can see the traffic behind the mentions. Our guide to [key strategies to rank on AI](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/07/key-strategies-to-rank-on-ai.html) has a tracking template you can copy.

| --- | --- | --- |

| Improve your ten strongest pages | 2 to 3 hours | High |
| Add direct answers, tables and FAQ data | 2 hours | Medium |
| Verify Bing Webmaster Tools and indexing | 30 minutes | Medium |
| Set up a monthly AI citation check | 1 hour | Medium |

## The Local Business Angle

A plumber is not a publisher in the ILC sense, and joining the collective will not change a local search result. The reason to care is the channel, not the check.

AI answer engines now handle questions like "who fixes a burst pipe near me" and "best dentist for nervous patients." Being in those answers sends people to your site or your phone, even when the answer never links. The mechanics of who gets picked are covered in [how AI picks local businesses](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/16/how-ai-picks-local-businesses-2026.html), and most of it comes down to consistent business data, real reviews and pages that answer specific questions.

A licensing check may never reach a local service business. A citation in an AI answer can send a customer to you this month.
