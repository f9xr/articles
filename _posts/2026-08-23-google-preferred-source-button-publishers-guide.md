If you publish content online, blog posts, local news, product guides, service pages, you've probably felt the squeeze as AI-generated answers swallow up clicks that used to land on your website. Google knows this is happening too. On August 20, 2026, it rolled out a major upgrade to a tool built specifically to help publishers win some of that direct traffic back.

The tool is called Preferred Sources, and the update is simple but useful. Publishers can now embed an interactive button directly on their own website, letting readers flag that site as a trusted source with one click, right where they're reading, instead of being sent off to a separate Google settings page.

Adoption numbers suggest readers are actually using this. Since the original Preferred Sources feature launched in May 2026, they have selected more than 600,000 unique sources, up from roughly 345,000 just a few months earlier. Google also shared a stat that explains why this matters: people are twice as likely to click through to a source they've marked as preferred compared to one they haven't.

For business owners trying to hold onto direct website traffic in an AI-shaped search environment, this tool deserves attention. Here's what changed, how it works, and how to add it to your own site.

## What Is Google's Preferred Sources Feature?

Before getting into the August update itself, it helps to understand the feature this builds on.

### The Original Launch in May 2026

Google first introduced Preferred Sources in May 2026, giving users a way to personalize their search experience by explicitly marking certain publications as trusted favorites. At launch, this worked through Google's own interface. A user would search for a news topic, click an icon next to Top Stories, search for and select their preferred sources, then refresh the results to see more links from those chosen sites surfacing more prominently.

The feature originally worked within Top Stories, then expanded in May to also influence AI Mode and AI Overviews. That expansion matters: a user's preferred sources could now shape which sites Google's AI-generated answers pulled from and cited, not just which links ranked in traditional results.

### What Changed on August 20, 2026

Mrinalini Loew, General Manager of Google's Search Ecosystem, announced the upgrade: an interactive Preferred Sources button that publishers can embed directly on their own web pages. Previously, marking a site as preferred required a reader to navigate away to Google's dedicated source preferences page and search for the site by name. Now a reader can click a button right there on the article or homepage they're already reading, confirm their choice, and land straight back on that same page rather than being routed elsewhere.

## Why Google Built This: The AI Traffic Problem

The motivation behind this matters if you're deciding whether to use the tool, so here it is plainly.

### AI Search Is Eating Into Referral Traffic

As AI-powered search tools increasingly answer questions directly inside the results page instead of sending users through to the publisher's site, referral traffic for many content creators has declined. [TechCrunch's coverage](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/20/google-gives-publishers-a-new-way-to-fight-ai-driven-traffic-losses/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} of the update was blunt about the framing, describing the button as something Google "threw" to publishers "negatively impacted" by exactly this shift.

We've covered the same pattern from the business side before, including [why zero-click search makes your Google Business Profile and homepage matter more than ever](https://f9xr.github.io/articles/2026/08/16/zero-click-search-google-business-profile-homepage.html). Preferred Sources is Google's most publisher-focused answer to that trend so far.

### A Recent Data Point Worth Knowing

Coverage of this announcement also referenced a separate finding from Pew Research: over one-third of web pages published since ChatGPT's late 2022 release show signs of AI authorship. That statistic describes the environment Google is working in, a web increasingly saturated with machine-written content, where both Google's ranking systems and its human users benefit from clearer signals about which sources people actually trust.

AI citation patterns are shifting quickly in general. When reporting showed [Reddit citations dropping sharply in ChatGPT answers](https://f9xr.github.io/articles/2026/08/21/reddit-chatgpt-citation-drop.html), it became clear that no source's visibility inside AI answers is guaranteed. Explicit reader signals like Preferred Sources give publishers a small lever they control.

### Google Needs This Data Too

There's another dynamic here, one that surfaced in industry discussion after the announcement. Google's ranking systems have historically leaned heavily on click behavior and engagement signals to work out which sources are high quality and trustworthy. As AI-generated answers reduce the number of clicks users make on individual links, Google loses some of that natural signal.

The Preferred Sources button hands Google a direct, explicit trust signal from users. It essentially invites people to manually state what the click-through data used to reveal on its own. [Search Engine Roundtable](https://www.seroundtable.com/google-preferred-source-button-update-41912.html){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} and [PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/google/2026/google-launches-preferred-source-button-for-publisher-websites){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} both flagged this tension in their coverage of the launch.

## How the Preferred Source Button Works for Readers

Understanding the reader-facing experience explains why publishers should care about adding it.

When a visitor lands on your website and clicks the embedded button, they're telling Google directly that your site is one they know and trust. Google then uses that explicit signal to personalize that reader's future results, feeds, and AI-generated answers, giving your content greater visibility whenever it's relevant to what that person searches for or asks about next.

This works across several Google surfaces at once:

* **Top Stories:** your content surfaces more prominently within news carousels
* **AI Overviews and AI Mode:** your site becomes more likely to be cited and linked when Google's AI generates an answer to a relevant query
* **Discover:** your content appears more often in that reader's personalized feed
* **Google News:** the same personalization applies within the dedicated News experience

The reader keeps full control throughout. They can add or remove preferred sources at any time through [Google's source preferences page](https://support.google.com/websearch/answer/16379181){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}, and the whole system is opt-in, based on explicit reader action rather than automatic tracking.

## How to Add the Preferred Source Button to Your Website

Here's a practical walkthrough for publishers and business owners ready to implement this.

### 1. Get the Embed Code From Google's Documentation

Google has published the official embed code through its [Search Central documentation](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/preferred-sources){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}. That's the authoritative source for correct, current code, not secondhand versions circulating elsewhere.

### 2. Place It Where Readers Will Actually See It

Think about placement before you paste anything. Prominent spots work best: near your homepage header, at the end of your most popular articles, or within your site navigation. Returning visitors and new readers both need a natural moment to notice it.

### 3. Confirm the Redirect Behavior

Part of what makes the August update useful is that clicking the button returns readers to the exact page they were on, instead of dumping them into a separate Google settings flow. Test this yourself after implementation so you know it behaves correctly on your site.

### 4. Pair It With a Clear Call to Action

This is a new interaction pattern for most readers. A short label near the button, something like "Add us as a preferred source on Google," helps people understand what clicking actually does before they commit.

### 5. Watch Search Console Over the Following Weeks

Keep an eye on your performance data after launch. Preferred Sources influence is personalized per reader rather than a universal ranking boost, so track Top Stories, Discover, and AI Overview visibility over time to judge whether the feature is moving traffic for your site. Our guide to [SEO metrics beyond rankings](https://f9xr.github.io/articles/2026/08/19/seo-metrics-beyond-rankings.html) covers how to read these signals sensibly.

### 6. Tell Your Existing Audience About It

Your most loyal readers, the ones already inclined to trust and return to your content, are the most likely to click once they know the button exists. A brief mention in a newsletter, social post, or on-site banner can lift adoption among exactly the audience most likely to help your visibility.

## Is It Actually Worth the Effort?

Honest assessment time. There are good reasons to implement this, and reasonable reasons to temper expectations.

### The Case For Implementing It

* **A confirmed click-through boost:** Google's own data shows people are twice as likely to click through to a preferred source. Even if only a modest share of your audience opts in, it changes how often those readers click.
* **Direct influence on AI answers, not just traditional search:** as more queries get answered inside AI Overviews and AI Mode, having an explicit mechanism to increase your visibility within those citations stands out. Few publisher-facing tools touch AI answers at all right now.
* **Low implementation cost:** this is a straightforward embed, not a development project. Smaller publishers and local businesses without dedicated developers can handle it.
* **Rapid adoption:** growing from 345,000 to over 600,000 unique preferred sources in a few months suggests real reader engagement, not a quietly ignored tool.

### The Case for Skepticism

* **It shifts responsibility onto publishers and readers.** A fair criticism making the rounds after the launch: this asks publishers and readers to manually do what Google's algorithm used to determine automatically through natural click behavior.
* **It won't fully solve declining AI-driven traffic.** Even Google's own framing treats this as one tool among several, not a fix for the broader referral decline publishers are experiencing.
* **Reader adoption requires active promotion.** The button does nothing on its own. Without promotion, most of your audience will never notice it exists.
