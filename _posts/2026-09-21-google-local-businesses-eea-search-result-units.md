If you run a local business anywhere in the European Economic Area, from a plumber in Berlin to a bakery in Lisbon, Google Search just changed how customers might find you, and it happened with barely any noise. On September 18, 2026, Google quietly updated its Search Central documentation to confirm that local business queries are now supported in two redesigned EEA search result units that first rolled out earlier that month.

This might sound like a small technical footnote, but for local businesses it matters. These two units, called the aggregator unit and the supplier unit, decide who gets prime visibility when someone in Europe searches for something like "plumber near me" or "bakery open now." One favors approved directories and comparison platforms. The other gives individual businesses a shot at visibility without needing to send Google any extra data at all.

If you're a local business owner, startup, or agency managing local SEO for clients in the EEA, this update directly affects how your business shows up on the results page. Here's exactly what changed, how the two units work, and what you should actually do about it.

## What Actually Changed on September 18

Google first documented two new EEA-only search result units on September 8, 2026: the **aggregator unit** and the **supplier unit**. At launch, these units covered hotels, flights, long-distance trains and buses, and products. Local business queries were not part of that initial list, though Google's supplier unit documentation did mention brick-and-mortar businesses and plumbers as illustrative examples.

Ten days later, on September 18, Google updated the documentation for both units and added local business queries to the list of supported query types. Google's own [changelog](https://developers.google.com/search/updates){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} described it in one short line: the aggregator unit and supplier unit now support local business queries. Industry coverage of the change, including [Search Engine Journal's report](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/google-adds-local-businesses-to-eea-search-result-units/589955/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} and [Search Engine Roundtable's writeup](https://www.seroundtable.com/google-local-business-query-units-42115.html){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}, treated it as a quiet expansion of the original rollout.

In practical terms, that means a local search in the EEA, for a plumber, a hair salon, a restaurant, or any brick-and-mortar business, can now trigger the same two-unit layout that previously only applied to travel and shopping searches. It's part of the wider restructuring of European search that we covered in our [look at Google's EU search revamp under the Digital Markets Act](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/08/google-eu-search-revamp-dma-fines.html), where aggregator-style units are becoming a recurring feature of the results page.

## How the Aggregator Unit Works

The aggregator unit is built for what Google calls Vertical Search Services, a category that includes:

* Online directories
* Comparison shopping services
* Metasearch engines
* Other aggregator-style platforms that list many businesses in one place

Here's what matters for local businesses: only one aggregator unit shows at a time on the results page. The top-ranked provider inside that unit is expanded by default, while other approved aggregators sit behind a dropdown the user has to click to see. Google's [documentation for the aggregator unit](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/aggregator-unit){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} lays out the layout in detail.

### How Aggregators Qualify

Getting into the aggregator unit isn't automatic. A platform has to:

1. Apply and get approved as a Vertical Search Service.
2. Have content that is directly relevant to the specific query category.
3. Supply data to Google through a direct data feed or a real-time API, depending on the query type.

For local business queries specifically, Google points aggregators to its [Local Point of Interest Feed documentation](https://developers.google.com/actions-center/verticals/lodging/reference/point-of-interest-feed){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} as the relevant data channel. So the platforms that feed this unit are expected to keep structured, up-to-date information flowing into Google, not just a one-time listing.

## How the Supplier Unit Works

The supplier unit is where individual businesses, not directories, get their own placement. This is the part local business owners should pay closest attention to.

The key detail: a business does **not** need to send Google any additional data feed to appear in the supplier unit. Google populates it based on information it can already crawl and understand about your business, similar in spirit to how a Google Business Profile or a well-structured website already feeds into local search results. The [supplier unit documentation](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/supplier-unit){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} makes that crawl-based model explicit.

There's a catch, though. The supplier unit only appears **alongside** an aggregator unit. If no aggregator unit is triggered for a given search, the supplier unit doesn't show either. That makes the two units a package deal rather than two fully independent placements.

Because the supplier unit leans on data Google already crawls, the structured data on your site becomes a direct input into whether you're represented correctly. Our guide on [structuring local business data for AI indexers](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/19/structuring-local-business-data-meta-ai-indexers.html) shows the actual schema fields and markup patterns, the same ones that decide whether a supplier unit renders your business the way you intend.

## Aggregator Unit vs. Supplier Unit: A Quick Comparison

| Feature | Aggregator Unit | Supplier Unit |
|---|---|---|
| **Who it's for** | Directories, comparison sites, metasearch platforms | Individual local businesses |
| **Data required** | Direct data feed or real-time API | No extra data beyond what Google already crawls |
| **Approval process** | Must apply and be approved as a Vertical Search Service | No separate approval process reported |
| **Visibility control** | Top provider expanded by default; others behind a dropdown | Appears only if an aggregator unit also appears |
| **Query types covered** | Hotels, flights, trains/buses, products, and now local businesses | Same categories, shown alongside the aggregator unit |
| **Region** | EEA only | EEA only |

## Why These EEA Search Units Matter for Local Businesses

### 1. Visibility Now Depends on More Than Just Your Google Business Profile

With local queries now flowing through this two-unit system, how your business appears may increasingly depend on both your own site and Business Profile data, and whether relevant directories in your industry or region have been approved as Vertical Search Services and are feeding accurate data about you into Google. Your Business Profile is still your foundation for the local pack and Google Maps, and our [Google Maps ranking playbook](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/16/rank-number-1-google-maps-2026.html) covers that side in depth, but this unit-based layout sits above it.

### 2. Being Listed on the Right Directories Could Matter More

If a directory or comparison platform relevant to your industry becomes an approved aggregator, appearing accurately and prominently on that platform could influence whether you show up at all in that aggregator's expanded results. This adds a new layer to local citation building beyond the usual directory listing advice, and it reframes which directories deserve your attention first.

### 3. It's Currently EEA Only, But Watch the Trend

Google's documentation is explicit that this applies only to the European Economic Area right now, it's not live in markets like Thailand, for example, and there's no confirmed timeline for wider rollout. That said, Google has a track record of testing region-specific search experiences before expanding them. The same pattern showed up with the EU's Digital Markets Act changes we [tracked in September](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/08/google-eu-search-revamp-dma-fines.html), so businesses outside the EEA should still keep an eye on this unit structure.

### 4. Accurate, Crawlable Business Information Is Now More Important Than Ever

Since the supplier unit pulls from what Google can already crawl, rather than a submitted feed, the accuracy and clarity of your website's business information, name, address, phone number, services, and structured data, plays a direct role in whether you're represented correctly. This is the same set of signals that decide [how AI pick local businesses](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/16/how-ai-picks-local-businesses-2026.html) when someone asks for a recommendation, so the effort serves both new-style result units and AI search at once.

## Practical Steps for EEA Local Businesses

### 1. Audit Your Google Business Profile

Make sure your business name, category, address, hours, and photos are complete and accurate. This remains foundational, whether or not the new units apply to your specific search category yet. Our guide to [zero-click search and your Google Business Profile](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/16/zero-click-search-google-business-profile-homepage.html) breaks down what "complete and accurate" actually means, and it's the reason F9XR's [Google Business optimization](https://f9xr.org/services/google-business-optimization.html) service exists.

### 2. Check Your Website's Local Business Structured Data

Implement or review your `LocalBusiness` schema markup. Since the supplier unit relies on crawlable data, clean structured data helps Google understand exactly what your business does and where it operates. The [structuring local business data guide](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/19/structuring-local-business-data-meta-ai-indexers.html) walks through the schema types and fields that matter.

### 3. Identify Relevant Aggregators in Your Industry

Look at which directories, comparison platforms, or metasearch services are active and well-regarded in your specific local industry, home services, hospitality, retail, and consider whether being well-represented there is worth prioritizing. If one of them gets approved as a Vertical Search Service, an accurate listing on that platform could put you in front of the expanded provider slot.

### 4. Monitor Your Search Appearance in the EEA

If you have access to international visibility tools or a local SEO partner, check how your business currently appears for relevant local queries. Search behavior and result layout can vary meaningfully between regions right now, so a tracking setup that only watches your home market will miss the EEA picture entirely.

### 5. Don't Panic, But Don't Ignore It Either

This is a documentation update, not a dramatic algorithm shake-up. But it does reflect a real shift in how Google structures local search results in the EEA, and local SEO strategies that account for it early will be better positioned as the rollout matures.
