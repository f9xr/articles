Search for "best accountant near me" or "best invoicing app for freelancers" and you will get lists. They are easy to scan and match how people shop, which is why so many businesses write them.

It is also why so many lists have turned into junk: ten items, the writer's own product at number one, the same cheerful adjectives, and an "Updated for 2026" label on a page nobody touched.

In 2026, that style stopped being a clever trick and became a risk. This guide shows you how to write a list that helps a real person decide, without stuffing, hidden ads, or pretending to be neutral when you are not.

> **Quick answer:** To write a listicle without spamming, pick a question real customers ask, set clear ranking criteria before choosing items, test or research each item first hand, include fair alternatives (including competitors), disclose every financial or ownership tie, and update the page when facts change. Do not rank yourself first without proof, do not mass produce lists with AI, and mark paid or affiliate links properly.

## What a Listicle Is, and Why People (and AI) Still Use Them

A listicle is an article built around a list: "7 ways to get more Google reviews," "5 tools to automate your invoices," "9 questions to ask before hiring a web designer." The format works because it matches how people decide. They want options, they want them compared, and they want to skim.

AI search tools like ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and Perplexity also like lists for a simple reason: a clear heading per item with a short, factual explanation is easy to read and quote. Google's AI Overviews and AI Mode pull from pages in similar ways.

The format is neutral. What matters is why the list exists.

## What Changed in 2026: Where Honest Lists End and Spam Begins

Google clarified that its [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} cover AI answers. On May 15, 2026, Google updated its spam policies to say they apply to generative AI responses in Google Search, including AI Overviews and AI Mode. [Search Engine Roundtable](https://www.seroundtable.com/google-spam-policies-apply-ai-responses-41331.html){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} reported the wording: Google said the rules apply to all of Google Search, including AI features. It did not invent new spam types.

Self-ranked "best of" lists drew scrutiny. Researcher [Lily Ray](https://lilyraynyc.substack.com/p/why-calling-yourself-the-best-could){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} analyzed 100 "best [category] software" queries in Google's AI Overviews across three dates between April and June 2026. Of the 80 prompts that showed an AI Overview, 323 self-promotional listicles were cited, and in 69% of those cases (224 of 323) the brand that wrote the list was left out of the actual recommendation. Her work also describes SaaS and B2B sites that lost a large share of organic visibility in early 2026 after relying on this tactic.

Caveats:

- This is one researcher's B2B software analysis. It has not been independently replicated, and Google has not named self-promotional listicles in any policy.
- Later reports describe higher exclusion rates by September, but we have not verified those figures, so treat them as directional.
- Numbers vary. [Peec AI](https://peec.ai/blog/self-promotional-listicles-analysis-from-232k-citations){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} analyzed 232,000 citations across 13,000 listicles, as reported by Outpace SEO, and found roughly 11% of AI citations came from self-promotional lists, with ChatGPT lower (around 4%) and Google AI Mode and Perplexity higher (around 10 to 11%).

For your business, the safest reading is simple: lists written to help readers hold up. Lists written to push your own product tend to fall apart and can drag your site down with them.

### Honest listicle vs spam listicle

| Question | Honest listicle | Spam listicle |
|---|---|---|
| Why was it written? | To help someone choose | To rank, sell or place links |
| Ranking criteria | Stated up front | Hidden or invented after the fact |
| Where does the writer rank? | Disclosed, and only if earned | Always first, with no proof |
| Evidence | Testing, data, screenshots, real experience | Generic claims and adjectives |
| Competitors | Included fairly, with real strengths | Missing, or described as inferior without proof |
| Money ties | Disclosed clearly and links marked | Hidden affiliate or paid placements |
| Production | Written and checked by a person | Mass produced, one template, many topics |
| Updates | Real changes with a date and note | Date bumped, text unchanged |

## 7 Spam Traps to Avoid

### 1. Ranking yourself first with no proof

If you sell the product, say so at the top. If you rank yourself first, explain why with evidence. If you cannot back it up, do not rank yourself first. A reader (and an AI system) can usually tell.

### 2. Keyword stuffing and repeated phrases

"Best SEO company, best SEO company in Hyderabad, best SEO company near me." Repeating a phrase does not help. Write the way people speak, and use related words and entities naturally instead.

### 3. Mass producing lists with AI

Publishing hundreds of near identical lists is the kind of scaled content that Google's policies target. One strong list you can stand behind beats fifty weak ones. If you use AI for outlining or editing, a person must verify every claim and add first hand knowledge. Our guide to [de-sloppifying your website](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/28/how-to-de-ai-slop-your-website.html) covers this.

### 4. Hidden affiliate links and paid spots

Money changes the advice, and readers deserve to know. State affiliate or sponsored relationships clearly, and mark those links with `rel="sponsored"` (or `rel="nofollow"` where appropriate). Check [Google's guide to qualifying outbound links](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/qualify-outbound-links){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} and the advertising rules in your own country. Our [affiliate disclosure](https://f9xr.org/articles/press/affiliate-disclosure.html) explains how we handle this on F9XR Articles.

### 5. Copying someone else's list

Taking another site's top ten, rewording it and publishing it adds nothing. If you cannot add testing, a new angle, local knowledge or better criteria, do not publish.

### 6. Fake freshness

Changing "2025" to "2026" in the title is not an update. Only change the date when you have changed something real, and say what changed.

### 7. Items nobody can verify

Invented businesses, vague "experts say" claims and statistics with no source erode trust fast. If you cannot link or explain where a fact came from, cut it. See our guide on [how to fact check and audit your website](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/10/04/how-to-fact-check-audit-your-website.html).

## How to Write a Listicle Step by Step

### Step 1: Start with a real question

Pick a question customers actually ask. Check your emails, WhatsApp messages, Google Business Profile questions and "People also ask" boxes. A good listicle answers one clear question: "What should I check before hiring a web designer?" beats "Top 10 web design tips."

Ask what the reader wants after reading: to decide, to learn, or to compare. That is intent, and we explain it in our guide to [decoding search intent](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/21/decoding-search-intent-what-customers-want.html).

### Step 2: Set your criteria before you pick items

Write down how you will judge each entry before choosing any. For example, for "best invoicing tools for small shops":

- Price for a small team
- Ease of setup
- GST or tax support (if relevant)
- Support quality
- Reliability and data export

Publish your criteria in a short section. It shows the list was built on a method, not on whoever paid or whoever you like.

### Step 3: Do the work first hand

Try the tool. Call the business. Read the documentation. Check the real price. Take screenshots or notes. Say what you did and did not do. "We tested the free plan for two weeks and did not test the paid add-ons" is honest and useful.

### Step 4: Include fair alternatives

A good list includes options that are not yours. If your product fits some readers, say who it fits and who it does not. Readers respect that, and so do systems that pick sources for AI answers.

### Step 5: Use a repeatable structure for every item

Give each entry the same shape so readers can compare quickly:

| Part | What to include |
|---|---|
| Heading | The item name and what it is best for |
| One line summary | The honest takeaway in under 30 words |
| What we checked | Your evidence, test or source |
| Good for | The type of reader who benefits |
| Watch out for | At least one real drawback |
| Price or cost | As shown on the official page, with the check date |

### Step 6: Disclose everything that matters

Add a short note near the top: who wrote this, whether you own or sell any listed item, whether you earn from links, and when you last checked the facts. Link to your public editorial policy. We explain how to set one up in our [editorial policy page guide](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/10/05/editorial-policy-page-eeat-guide.html).

### Step 7: Add a comparison table and a short FAQ

A table lets readers compare in seconds. A short FAQ answers follow-up questions. Keep answers direct, around 40 to 60 words, and match your schema markup. See our post on [whether your blog needs an FAQ section](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/22/does-your-blog-need-faq-section.html).

### Step 8: Edit for a human voice

Read the draft out loud. Cut filler openers, repeated adjectives and anything that could appear on a hundred other sites. Add a detail only you know: a customer story, a mistake, a price you paid. Nobody can copy that.

### Step 9: Publish with a clear update plan

Put the "last checked" date near the top. Review prices, links and facts every three to six months, and add a change note when you update. Give readers and listed businesses a way to report errors.

## A Copyable Item Template

Start every entry like this:

```
### 3. [Name]: best for [specific reader]

One line summary in plain words.

What we checked: [test, call, documentation, price page, date]
Good for: [who will like it]
Watch out for: [real drawback]
Price: [amount as shown on the official page, checked on DATE]
```

Done well:

> **3. Ledgerly: best for one-person shops that hate setup**
> Fast to start, light on features. We created a test account, sent three sample invoices and exported the data to a spreadsheet. It does not handle inventory. Price shown on its pricing page when we checked on November 3: [amount].

Done badly:

> **3. Ledgerly: the ultimate game changing invoicing solution!**
> Ledgerly is a powerful, robust and seamless tool that will transform your business.

The first version gives readers something to act on. The second could describe anything. The name is invented; copy the structure.

## Listicle Ideas for Local Businesses

Local businesses can use lists well because customers want local, specific help. These ideas do not require you to rank yourself first:

- "7 questions to ask before hiring a [plumber, tutor, photographer] in [city]." This helps buyers and shows expertise.
- "5 mistakes people make when [choosing a gym, buying a used bike, renewing a license]." Teach first, sell later.
- "Our checklist for [a good website, a safe wedding venue, a healthy kitchen]." A checklist is a list, and it earns trust.
- "Where to find [local resource]: 8 places we actually use." Only include places you have used.
- Local roundups of other businesses. Contact each one, confirm details and tell them it is coming. Do not invent entries.

If you include your own business in a "best of" list, say so: "We run one of the businesses on this list. Here is how we judged all of them." ## Optimizing a Listicle for Google and AI Search

Honest does not mean invisible. Good structure helps readers and search systems.

- One clear H2 or H3 per item, with the item's name and who it is for
- A short answer first under each heading, then the detail
- A comparison table with real attributes (price, best for, limits)
- Author details and dates so readers know who wrote it and when it was checked
- Related terms used naturally, such as the category, location, brands and common questions
- Structured data such as FAQPage markup for your FAQ, and [ItemList](https://schema.org/ItemList){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} where it fits. Keep markup matched to the visible page
- Clean internal links to related guides, including our post on [how to write SEO friendly articles](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/09/how-to-write-seo-friendly-articles.html)

For more on trust signals, see our [E-E-A-T checklist](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/04/google-e-e-a-t-checklist-every-seo-should-bookmark.html). Track organic clicks, AI citations and calls or form fills that start from the page, not just rankings. Our post on [SEO metrics beyond rankings](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/19/seo-metrics-beyond-rankings.html) goes deeper.
