Picture a small restaurant owner on a busy Friday. A customer finds the place on Google Maps, taps "Order online," and lands on a delivery app the owner never really chose. The app takes its cut, the customer sees a competitor's suggestions on the next screen, and the owner finds out only when the numbers come in at the end of the month.

For years, this has been one of the most common complaints from restaurant operators. This week, Google did something about it. Restaurants can now turn off specific third-party ordering providers straight from their Google Business Profile dashboard, instead of chasing each platform with a removal request.

If you run a restaurant, a cloud kitchen, a cafe, or any local food business, this is a change worth understanding properly. Below we cover what Google announced, how the old process worked, how to use the new control, and the checks to run before you flip any switch.

> **Quick answer:** Google now lets restaurant owners manage and opt out of specific third-party ordering providers directly from the Food ordering section of their Google Business Profile, which controls what shows on Google Search and Maps. The control has separate options for pickup and delivery. Previously, owners had to ask each provider to remove its link, and providers had up to five business days to act.

## What Google announced

The news came from Lisa Landsman, Google's Head of Industry Engagement and SMB Success, in a LinkedIn post that <a href="https://www.searchenginejournal.com/google-restaurants-opt-out-ordering-providers/594794/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Search Engine Journal</a> and <a href="https://www.seroundtable.com/google-business-profiles-ordering-controls-42411.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Search Engine Roundtable</a> picked up on October 28, 2026. Here are the main points.

* Restaurants can manage and opt out of specific ordering providers from their Business Profile dashboard.
* The provider list shows up under the Food ordering section.
* A "Manage provider" panel lets you control each provider.
* The screenshot Landsman shared shows separate switches for pickup and delivery, so you can keep a provider for one and drop it for the other.
* Google framed it as a response to one of the most frequent requests it hears from restaurant operators: more control over ordering links.

Google also offered two pieces of advice alongside the announcement. Keeping a mix of trusted providers can still work in your favor, and owners should review their active ordering channels regularly to make sure every link leads to an accurate menu and a smooth experience.

A Google product manager said in the post's comments that the setting is available in all countries, according to Search Engine Journal. Google's help page still described the older removal request process when we checked, so the rollout may be gradual. If you do not see the new control today, check again over the coming days.

## How it worked before, and what changes now

| Item | Before | Now |
|---|---|---|
| Who removes a provider link | The ordering provider | You, from your dashboard |
| How you asked | Removal form, support ticket, or the provider's own opt-out method | A switch in the Business Profile |
| Waiting time | Providers had up to five business days to complete a request | Handled by you directly, with no wait on the provider's process |
| Granularity | Often all or nothing, depending on the provider's process | Separate controls for pickup and delivery |
| Where to look | Provider sites plus Google | One place: Food ordering in your profile |

The old process came from Google's compliance policy for ordering providers, which placed the burden on each provider to give restaurants a way to remove themselves from the provider's feed to Google. That is why so many owners found themselves emailing support teams and waiting.

Google's <a href="https://support.google.com/business/answer/10842217?hl=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">help page on managing online ordering options</a> also explains why links appear in the first place. Providers who say they have an authorized relationship with your business are listed on your profile automatically. Integrations tend to switch on when a provider is first set up, and many owners never revisit the list.

## Why this matters for restaurant owners

### 1. Margins

Third-party platforms charge commissions and fees, and the rates vary by provider and plan. Google's own announcement acknowledged that a provider may stop suiting a restaurant's operations or margins. You can act on that today without a long back-and-forth.

### 2. Customer relationship

When someone orders through a marketplace, that platform usually owns the customer data and the follow-up. When someone orders from your own site, you keep the relationship, the email address, and the chance to bring them back.

### 3. Menu accuracy

Third-party pages can show outdated prices, old photos, or items you no longer sell. Fewer providers means fewer places to keep correct.

### 4. Brand control

The ordering link on Google is a high-intent moment. Whoever sits behind that button shapes the customer's first impression of your restaurant.

## How to use the new control

Google's interface can change, and the rollout may be gradual, so treat the steps below as a guide and follow whatever your dashboard shows.

1. Sign in to the Google account that manages your Business Profile and open the profile for the restaurant.
2. Open Food ordering. This is where Google lists the ordering options and providers on your profile.
3. Review the full provider list. Write down every provider and whether it is set for pickup, delivery, or both.
4. Open the "Manage provider" panel for the provider you want to change.
5. Choose pickup, delivery, or both, and opt out where it makes sense for your business.
6. Check the live listing. Search your restaurant name on Google and on Maps (ideally on your phone) and confirm the button and links now look the way you want.
7. Repeat for each location. If you manage more than one restaurant profile, each one needs its own review.

Google's help page also notes that turning off "Accept orders on your profile" hides any third-party options and links you added, so be careful not to flip that by accident while you are tidying up.

## Before you switch anything off: six checks

Removing a link is quick. Recovering lost orders is not. Run through these first.

1. Read your contract. Turning off a link on Google does not by itself end your agreement with a platform. You may still have terms, fees, or exclusivity clauses to deal with. Check before you assume anything.
2. Look at your order data. How many orders come from each provider? Which ones bring new customers, and which ones mostly take orders you would have received anyway?
3. Make sure direct ordering works. Test your own ordering page on a phone, on slow mobile data, from search to payment. If it is clunky, you will lose orders.
4. Set your preferred option. Google lets you mark a preferred provider for pickup and delivery. Point it to your own ordering page where you can.
5. Check your delivery capacity. If you have no drivers of your own, you may still need a platform for delivery even if you drop it for pickup.
6. Plan a test period. Change one thing at a time and watch orders for two to four weeks, so you know what caused any change.

### A simple decision guide

| Your situation | A sensible approach |
|---|---|
| Strong direct ordering page, own delivery staff | Make your link preferred, consider opting out of the providers you rarely use |
| Good pickup volume, no delivery drivers | Push direct pickup, keep one trusted delivery provider |
| New restaurant still building awareness | Keep a mix of providers for visibility while you build your own channel |
| Provider with high fees and low volume | Strong candidate to opt out, after checking your contract |
| Provider showing wrong menu or prices | Fix the feed with them first, or opt out if they cannot correct it |

## Pickup and delivery: why the split matters

The separate controls are the most practical part of this update. Pickup and delivery have very different economics.

With pickup, the customer does the last mile. Direct ordering usually makes the most sense here, since you are not paying for a driver and a platform's reach matters less once someone already knows your name. Delivery is harder: drivers, routes, and timing take work. Many small restaurants rely on a platform here, and that can be a fair trade.

An example (illustrative, not a case study): a family-run biryani restaurant might switch off third-party pickup links, because most pickup customers are regulars who already have the phone number, while keeping one delivery partner so evening orders still reach people across town.

## Build the channel you are pointing to

Opting out only helps if there is a good place for customers to go instead. Your direct ordering setup needs a fast mobile page with an "Order now" button people can see, a current menu with real prices and photos, and a simple checkout that accepts the payment methods your customers already use. It should also show clear opening hours, address, and contact details, plus local business schema so search engines read your information correctly.

## A weekly Business Profile audit for restaurants

Google itself recommended regular reviews of ordering channels. Make it a ten-minute habit.

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Ordering links | Every link opens the right page and the right restaurant |
| Menu | Prices, items, and availability match your current menu |
| Hours | Holiday and special hours are updated |
| Photos | Recent, accurate, and not showing dishes you no longer serve |
| Providers | No old or unknown providers listed |
| Preferred option | Still points to your direct ordering page |
| Reviews | New reviews answered, especially about ordering problems |

## Common mistakes to avoid

The biggest mistake is opting out of everything at once, before your own channel is ready. Do not assume the Google switch cancels your contract; it does not necessarily. Review every location, remove old providers that send customers to outdated menus, test on mobile, and view your live listing after each change.
