Every rupee or dollar you point at a weak channel is money your strongest channel never got to spend. That single line is the whole problem with the network toggles in Google Ads, and it has been the problem for years.

Run a Search campaign for a small business and you face a binary choice. Leave the Google Display Network and Search partners switched on and risk paying for clicks that never call. Switch them off and hope you are not walking away from cheap conversions. There was rarely a middle setting.

On October 14, 2026, Google added that middle setting. Two optional controls, channel prioritization and budget allocation, let you rank channels as High priority or Low priority and put a spending ceiling on the lower ones. [Google's own framing](https://blog.google/products/ads-commerce/channel-prioritization-budget-allocation/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} is that this moves advertisers past all or nothing channel targeting, so they can test secondary channels without betting the whole budget.

There is a date attached to it too. In February 2027, the current spend allocation setup for Search campaigns using the Display Network goes away. If you run those campaigns, that date shapes part of your plan whether you act or not.

> Quick answer: Channel prioritization lets you mark channels or channel groups as High or Low priority so budget fills the high priority ones first. Budget allocation lets you cap Low priority channels at a maximum percentage of your daily budget. Both are optional, apply across Search, Shopping, Performance Max, and Demand Gen, and roll out globally over the coming months.

## Channel prioritization: what Google announced on October 14

Google Ads is adding two connected features. They build on earlier control updates such as [Smart Bidding Exploration](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/15489627){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} and [campaign total budgets](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/10486938){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}, and they give advertisers more say in where money flows across Google's own inventory and partner networks.

### Channel prioritization

You can now categorize inventory as High priority or Low priority for specific channels or channel groupings. When that is set, campaigns fund the high priority channels first, staying within your target if you use Target CPA or Target ROAS, and only then send whatever budget remains to the lower priority channels.

The setup differs by campaign type:

- Search campaigns: Google Search, Search partners, and the Google Display Network can sit in different priority tiers.
- Performance Max and Demand Gen campaigns: Search, YouTube, Maps, Gmail, and Discover are grouped as a single tier called Google channels.
- Where it applies: account and campaign levels, and it works with [shared budgets](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/10487241){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}. Priority tiers can run across Search, Shopping, Performance Max, and Demand Gen.

Google's post includes a table showing which groupings you can adjust per campaign type. Read that table in the original announcement, because the options are not identical across campaign types.

### Flexible budget allocation

Once a channel or channel group is set to Low priority, you can define the maximum percentage of your daily budget it may use. Google describes this as best effort, which is the honest way to say it is a guardrail, not a hard stop.

This control matters most when a campaign uses Target CPA or Target ROAS and is not limited by budget. In that case prioritization alone changes little, because there is no budget shortage forcing a choice between channels. A percentage cap gives you a lever that works anyway. Think of it as a cost center with a fixed ceiling rather than an open line.

### The February 2027 change to plan for

Starting in February 2027, for Search campaigns with the Display Network on, the current spend allocation setup is retired. Advertisers may see spend shift across channels. To hold your current configuration, Google says to set the Display Network to Low priority with a 50% budget allocation.

If you run Search campaigns with the Display Network enabled, treat this as a deadline rather than a footnote. The checklist below turns it into a few concrete checks.

## Old way versus new way

| Question | Before | With the new controls |
|---|---|---|
| Can I test the Display Network or Search partners safely? | Mostly on or off | Yes, with a Low priority label and a percentage cap |
| What if I opted out years ago after poor results? | Stay out, or take a full risk to retest | Retest with a defined limit |
| Who decides which channel gets budget first? | The system, with little input from you | You name the high and low priority channels |
| Does it work with Target CPA and Target ROAS? | Limited options | Yes, though the cap is what helps when budget is not the constraint |
| Does it apply to shared budgets? | Not applicable | Yes |

## Why channel prioritization matters when the budget is small

Small advertisers run on a single scarce input: cash. When part of it is committed to a poor channel, the best channel simply goes without. The new controls change that math in four ways.

1. Safer experiments. A secondary channel gets a small, capped share, so you can read the results without funding the whole bet.
2. Protected core spend. Your best channel is funded first, so your highest intent searches are less likely to run dry midway through the day.
3. Cleaner peak planning. Google pointed to peak season specifically. With the holidays in view, you can decide in advance where extra demand money should land.
4. A budget ceiling someone can sign off on. A cap gives an owner or finance lead a clear number to approve.

### A worked example (illustrative)

Take a dental clinic on Target CPA. Today the Display Network runs on settings someone chose years ago and nobody has revisited. With the new controls, the clinic could set Google Search as High priority so "dentist near me" queries are funded first, set Search partners as Low priority with a small cap such as 10 percent to test it, and set the Display Network to a cap that matches its current spend, then trim it if call quality disappoints.

The percentages here are an illustration of how a test might be structured. They are not recommendations from Google, and your own data should set the numbers.

Now take an online store on Performance Max. Because Google surfaces are grouped as Google channels, the store's real decision is how much non Google inventory it will accept, and it can watch results by channel group before changing anything.

## How to prepare: a practical checklist

### Step 1: Audit where the spend goes now

Pull spend by network for each campaign. On Search campaigns, separate Google Search, Search partners, and the Display Network, and write the percentages down. You need a before picture to judge any after.

### Step 2: Flag the campaigns exposed to February 2027

Find the Search campaigns with the Display Network turned on. These are the ones Google says may see a spend shift once the current allocation setup retires.

### Step 3: Decide whether to keep the current behavior

If the Display Network performs for you today, Google's guidance is to set it to Low priority with a 50% budget allocation to hold the current setup. If it does not perform, this is a fair moment to lower the cap or drop it from the plan.

### Step 4: Define success before you test

Pick the numbers that matter to the business. For lead generation, that is often cost per qualified lead, call quality, or booked appointments, not clicks. Our guide to [SEM metrics in 2026](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/23/sem-metrics-complete-guide-2026.html) can help you choose the right ones.

### Step 5: Change one variable at a time

Set a modest cap on a single secondary channel, keep everything else steady, and let it run long enough to mean something. If you move bids, creative, and budget together, you will not know which change produced the result.

### Step 6: Review, then adjust the cap

After a few weeks, compare channel level results against your definition of success. If a Low priority channel brings quality conversions at a fair cost, raise its cap in steps. If it does not, lower it or turn it off.

A few habits keep the numbers honest. Do not set caps in the dark: give the channel a few weeks of existing data first. Remember that allocation is best effort, so watch actual spend rather than assuming the cap is exact. And track conversion quality, not only volume, because cheap clicks from a low intent placement can look fine on a report and do nothing for the business. ## What we still do not know

A few practical details sit with the Help Center and with live testing.

- Exact timing in your account. Google says the features roll out over the coming months, so they may not appear immediately.
- How tightly spend follows the caps. Google calls allocation best effort. Live account data will show how closely it holds.
- The exact channel groupings per campaign type. The detail lives in Google's post and Help Center, and it is worth reading before you plan changes.
- How the February 2027 shift looks in practice. Expect to monitor performance closely around that month.

Because the feature is new, check Google's [Help Center page on channel prioritization and budget allocation](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/18385152){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} for the latest instructions before you change anything.

## Where this sits in the wider ad picture

This update follows a clear pattern. Google keeps handing advertisers more controls while the systems underneath stay automated and AI driven. We have seen the same move from [Microsoft Advertising's AI Max rollout](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/20/microsoft-advertising-ai-max-global-rollout.html), the arrival of [ads inside ChatGPT](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/22/chatgpt-ads-guide-business-owners.html), and Google's own [retirement of language targeting](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/08/17/google-ads-language-targeting-retired-what-now.html). Automation is not retreating, and the people who understand the controls will keep getting more from it.

The return math is not exotic either. A cap turns a vague worry about wasted spend into a number you can watch, and that is the same discipline you would apply to any other line item. It is also a quieter way to compete: you do not need a larger budget to spend more wisely, which is the argument behind [outsmarting competitors without increasing your ad budget](https://f9xr.org/articles/2026/09/15/outsmart-competitors-without-increasing-ad-budget.html).

## Conclusion

The new controls will not rescue a weak campaign. What they do is give a small advertiser something rare in paid search: room to test without panic. You can keep your best channel funded, hand a secondary channel a small and measurable share, and replace old assumptions with your own data.
